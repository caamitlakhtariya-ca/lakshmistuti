The verse

॥ ध्यानश्लोकः ॥

बिल्वाटवीमध्यलसत्सरोजे
सहस्रपत्रे सुखसन्निविष्टाम्।
अष्टापदाम्भोरुहपाणिपद्मां
सुवर्णवर्णां प्रणमामि लक्ष्मीम्॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Bilvāṭavī-madhya-lasat-saroje
Sahasra-patre sukha-sanniviṣṭām;
Aṣṭāpadāmbhoruha-pāṇi-padmāṁ
Suvarṇa-varṇāṁ praṇamāmi Lakṣmīm.

Meaning in short

I bow to Lakshmi, golden in colour, seated at ease on a thousand-petalled lotus that shines in the middle of a grove of bilva trees, holding a golden lotus in her lotus-like hand.

A picture to hold in the mind: the golden Mother, seated at ease on a thousand-petalled lotus in a grove of bilva trees.

Key highlights

  • Bilva Grove: the sacred bael tree of Lakshmi
  • Sahasrapatra Lotus: a thousand petals, beyond counting
  • Sukha Ease: she is seated at rest and at peace
  • Suvarṇa Gold: her colour and the lotus in her hand

Explore the four pictures

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

BilvaGrove

The bilva, or bael tree, is counted as dear to Lakshmi. In the Śrī Sūktam the tree is said to have arisen from her radiance, and its three-part leaf is offered in worship. The verse places her in the middle of a whole grove of them. A reflection: she is pictured among living, growing things, not in a vault.

Pause and reflect: What grows quietly around me that I take for granted?

SahasrapatraLotus

A lotus with a thousand petals is an image of fullness. The lotus rises from mud and opens above the water, and the tradition uses it again and again for Lakshmi. A reflection: a thousand petals can be read as an invitation to a life that keeps opening.

Pause and reflect: Which part of my life is still opening?

SukhaEase

Sukha-sanniviṣṭām is a lovely phrase: seated well, seated at ease. The Goddess of prosperity is shown at rest, not anxious or grasping. A reflection: if she is at ease, perhaps prosperity is meant to be held calmly too.

Pause and reflect: Where am I holding wealth, or the wish for it, too tightly?

SuvarṇaGold

She holds a golden lotus in a hand that is itself like a lotus, and her colour is gold. The hymn is named after a stream of gold, and here is the same gold, still and shining. A reflection: the gold of the picture is radiance, not a heap of coins.

Pause and reflect: What would a calm, shining kind of richness look like for me?

Notes on each word
बिल्वाटवीमध्यलसत्सरोजेBilvāṭavī-madhya-lasat-saroje

Bilva is the bael tree, aṭavī is a forest or grove, madhya is the middle, lasat is shining, and saroja is a lotus (literally “born of a pool”). The ending -e marks the place: “in” or “on”. So the verse opens by setting a scene: a grove of bilva trees, and in the middle of it a shining lotus.

सहस्रपत्रेSahasra-patre

Sahasra is a thousand and patra is a petal or leaf. It describes the same lotus, so the Goddess sits on a lotus with a thousand petals. A thousand is the Sanskrit way of saying “beyond counting”.

सुखसन्निविष्टाम्Sukha-sanniviṣṭām

Sukha is ease or happiness, and sanniviṣṭa is settled, seated well. The ending -ām is the accusative: this is the Goddess whom the speaker bows to. She is not shown standing or hurrying, but at rest.

अष्टापदाम्भोरुहपाणिपद्माम्Aṣṭāpadāmbhoruha-pāṇi-padmām

Aṣṭāpada is a Sanskrit word for gold. Ambhoruha is a lotus (“growing in water”), pāṇi is a hand, and padma is a lotus. I read the compound as “she whose lotus-like hand has a golden lotus”. Some readers take aṣṭa as “eight” and link it to the eight forms of Lakshmi; I keep to the plain dictionary sense of gold, since the next word also speaks of gold.

सुवर्णवर्णाम्Suvarṇa-varṇām

Suvarṇa is “of good colour”, hence gold, and varṇa is colour. She is described as golden-hued, the same colour as the gold of the hymn’s name, kanaka.

प्रणमामिPraṇamāmi

Pra- adds intensity to the root nam, “to bow”. This is the only verb in the verse. All the long description before it exists to say who is being bowed to.

लक्ष्मीम्Lakṣmīm

The name arrives in the very last word. The listener is led through a grove, a lotus, a posture, a hand and a colour, and only then is the Goddess named.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

Before we begin

This is not a twenty-third verse. After the closing benediction in Verse 22, some editions add one more śloka, a short picture of the Goddess to hold in the mind. It is placed here as an appendix, outside the numbered verses.

Where the 22 verses praise her glance, her compassion and her names, this verse simply shows her: the place, the seat, the posture, the hand and the colour. It is a verse for seeing rather than asking.

“I bow to Lakshmi, golden in colour, seated at ease on a thousand-petalled lotus that shines in the middle of a grove of bilva trees, holding a golden lotus in her lotus-like hand.”

1A more poetic rendering

In the heart of a bilva grove a lotus shines, a lotus of a thousand petals; and there, at ease, sits the Mother, golden in hue, a golden lotus in her lotus hand. To Lakṣmī I bow.

A grammatical point: the whole verse is one long description that ends in a single verb, praṇamāmi, “I bow”. The Goddess is named only in the last word, Lakṣmīm. Everything before it builds the picture.

2The four pictures in the verse

Image 1 – Bilvāṭavī: a grove of bilva trees

Bilvāṭavī is a bilva grove. The bilva (bael) is a sacred tree in many Indian traditions, best known for its three-part leaf offered to Shiva. It is also linked to Lakshmi: the Śrī Sūktam says the tree, the bilva, arose from her radiance and austerity, and the tree is sometimes called śrī-vṛkṣa, “Lakshmi’s tree”.

So the setting is not a palace or a treasury. It is a quiet grove, with her own tree around her. The picture places prosperity among living, growing things.

Contemplation: Prosperity that is rooted in something living tends to be steadier than prosperity that sits only in a vault.

Image 2 – Sahasra-patra: the thousand-petalled lotus

Sahasra-patra is “thousand-petalled”. The number is not a count. It means fullness, a flower so open that the petals cannot be numbered. The lotus is Lakshmi’s own seat in the tradition, and it grows from mud yet stays clean above the water.

Later yogic writing also speaks of a thousand-petalled lotus at the crown of the head. That is a different tradition and the verse does not say so, so I mention it only as a reflection that the same number carries a sense of completeness in more than one place.

Image 3 – Aṣṭāpadāmbhoruha-pāṇi-padmā: the golden lotus in her hand

Here the compound is long. Aṣṭāpada is gold, ambhoruha is a lotus, pāṇi-padma is the lotus of the hand. She holds a golden lotus in a hand that is itself like a lotus. The image of a lotus held in a lotus is a way of saying that everything about her is of one grace.

One caution about the reading. Some people explain aṣṭa as the eight forms of Lakshmi (Aṣṭalakṣmī). That is a lovely thought, but I could not find it supported by the word itself, because aṣṭāpada is a known word for gold. I have kept to the plain meaning and offer the eight forms only as a possible reflection.

Contemplation: Gold held in an open hand looks different from gold held in a clenched one.

Image 4 – Suvarṇa-varṇā: golden in colour

Suvarṇa is gold and varṇa is colour, so she is golden in hue. The Śrī Sūktam begins by calling her hiraṇya-varṇā, also golden. The Kanakadhāra Stotram takes its name from kanaka, gold, and dhārā, stream.

This verse ends with that same colour, now still and shining. Together with sukha-sanniviṣṭām, “seated at ease”, it shows gold as radiance and calm.

Where this verse comes from

This verse is not one of the 22 verses of the standard edition. It is a meditation verse (dhyāna śloka) that some printed editions and websites place with the Kanakadhāra Stotram, and it is described in some accounts as one of a few extra verses said to come from palm-leaf manuscripts. I have not been able to trace it to a scholarly critical edition, so please treat its place in the hymn as a matter of tradition, and check the text against your own book.

3What the picture teaches: three lessons

1
Prosperity can be calm.

The Goddess is sukha-sanniviṣṭā, seated at ease. The hymn that began with a stream of gold ends with a picture of rest. Seeking prosperity does not have to mean living in restlessness.

2
A prayer can be a picture.

Most of the Stotram asks, praises and surrenders. This verse only describes. In many traditions, holding a form in the mind in this way is itself a prayer, a way of quietening the attention before or after reciting.

3
Gold and the lotus belong together.

The verse brings wealth (gold) and purity (the lotus) into one image. A life of prosperity that stays open, clear and unattached is the picture the hymn keeps returning to.

These are my own reflections inspired by the verse. They are not statements in the text.

5The central thought

See her before you ask her. The Bilvāṭavī verse gives a picture to hold in the mind: the golden Mother, seated at ease on a thousand-petalled lotus, among the trees that are dear to her.

Whether or not it belongs to the original hymn, it matches the hymn’s spirit. The stream of gold ends where it began, in grace that is quiet, bright and unhurried.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1Sit comfortably and let the breath settle.
  2. Minute 2Picture a grove of green trees, and in its middle a large lotus, open and shining.
  3. Minute 3See the Mother seated there, golden and at ease, a golden lotus in her hand. Stay with the picture without asking for anything.
  4. Minute 4Say Lakṣmīm praṇamāmi, “I bow to Lakshmi”, and let the picture fade.

Reflection for today

Where in my life could I rest a little, as the Mother rests on the lotus, instead of grasping?

The hymn ends, as it began, with grace. A picture of a golden Mother seated at ease is a good thing to carry out of the Stotram into the day.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
