The verse

॥ श्लोक ५ ॥

बाह्वन्तरे मधुजितः श्रितकौस्तुभे या
हारावलीव हरिनीलमयी विभाति।
कामप्रदा भगवतोऽपि कटाक्षमाला
कल्याणमावहतु मे कमलालयायाः॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Bāhvantare madhujitaḥ śrita-kaustubhe yā,
Hārāvalīva harinīlamayī vibhāti;
Kāmapradā bhagavato 'pi kaṭākṣa-mālā,
Kalyāṇam āvahatu me kamalālayāyāḥ.

Meaning in short

May the garland of sidelong glances of Lakshmi, whose home is the lotus, bring welfare to me: the garland which shines like a necklace of sapphires on the chest of Madhujit (Vishnu), where the Kaustubha jewel rests, and which grants wishes even to the Lord himself.

Her glances become a necklace of sapphires on Vishnu’s chest, and the poet asks that they bring welfare to him.

Key highlights

  • Hāra Necklace: glances shining like a string of sapphires
  • Kaustubha Jewel: beside the Kaustubha on Vishnu’s chest
  • Kāmapradā Wishes: a glance that grants wishes, even to the Lord
  • Kalyāṇa Welfare: the prayer for goodness, not only money

Explore the four images

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

HāraNecklace

The poet turns the picture around: her necklace is made of her own glances, worn on Vishnu. Each loving look is a blue gem, and a row of looks becomes a string of jewels.

Pause and reflect: What are the small, repeated kindnesses that make up my own necklace?

KaustubhaJewel

The Kaustubha is the jewel said to have risen at the churning of the ocean, the same churning in which Lakshmi arose. The verse places her glances beside it, on the chest of Madhujit.

Pause and reflect: What do I treasure that I would not call a possession?

KāmapradāWishes

Kāmapradā means giver of wishes. A devotional reading is that her grace is inseparable from Vishnu’s power to sustain the world. Wishes are many: wealth, but also security and peace.

Pause and reflect: If my wishes were all granted, which would matter most?

KalyāṇaWelfare

The last word asks for kalyāṇa: goodness, auspiciousness, welfare. A life can have money and still lack kalyāṇa. The poet asks for the larger thing.

Pause and reflect: Where in my life is welfare missing even though money is not?

Notes on each word
बाह्वन्तरेBāhv-antare

Bāhu is the arm; antara is the space within or between. The space between the arms is the chest. When bāhu meets antare, the u turns into v: bāhv-antare. The ending means “in, on”.

मधुजितःMadhu-jitaḥ

Madhu is a demon, who in tradition was slain by Vishnu together with Kaiṭabha. Jit is one who conquers. So Madhujit is “the conqueror of Madhu”, a name of Vishnu. The ending marks “of”.

श्रितकौस्तुभेŚrita-kaustubhe

Śrita is one who has taken shelter or settled; Kaustubha is the famous jewel of Vishnu, which tradition says rose from the churning of the ocean and rests on his chest. The word describes the chest: the chest on which the Kaustubha has settled.

याYā

A feminine “which”. It looks ahead to kaṭākṣa-mālā, the garland of glances, in the third line, and tells us the first two lines are describing that garland.

हारावलीवHārāvalī iva

Hāra is a necklace or string; āvalī is a row or line; iva is “like”. The words run together as hārāvalīva. A row of jewels strung along the chest.

हरिनीलमयीHari-nīla-mayī

Nīla is blue; harinīla is the name of a blue gem, usually rendered sapphire. -Mayī means “made of, full of”, and its feminine ending agrees with the garland. So the glances are a necklace of deep-blue jewels.

विभातिVibhāti

Bhā is to shine; the prefix vi adds brilliance. This is a plain statement of how the garland looks: it shines. The wish does not come until the last line.

कामप्रदाKāma-pradā

Kāma is desire, wish (it can also mean love); prada is giver. The feminine ending agrees with the garland: the garland of glances is the giver.

भगवतः अपिBhagavato ’pi

Bhagavat is the Blessed Lord, here Vishnu. Api is “even”. The two join as bhagavato ’pi. The surprise is in the word “even”: her glance is a giver of wishes to the Lord himself.

कटाक्षमालाKaṭākṣa-mālā

Kaṭākṣa is a sidelong, gracious glance; mālā is a garland. This is not a physical necklace but a garland made of glances, the heart of the verse.

कल्याणम् आवहतु मेKalyāṇam āvahatu me

Kalyāṇa is what is good and auspicious: welfare, well-being. Ā-vah is to bring; āvahatu is “let it bring, may it bring”. Me is “to me”. The words join as kalyāṇam āvahatu me. This is the wish of the whole verse.

कमलालयायाःKamalālayāyāḥ

Kamala is the lotus; ālaya is a dwelling. So the one whose dwelling is the lotus is Lakshmi. The ending marks “of”: the garland of glances of Lakshmi.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, your loving glances shine on Vishnu’s chest like a string of blue sapphires beside the Kaustubha jewel, and they give joy even to the Lord himself. May that same garland of your glances bring goodness into my life.” The first three lines are a picture; only the last line is the prayer.

From the text

What the verse actually says

The grammar is simple once the long compound is unpacked. The subject is the garland of glances. The first two lines say where it shines and how it looks: on the chest of Madhujit, like a sapphire necklace. The third line says what it does: it grants wishes even to the Lord. The wish itself is only in the last line: kalyāṇam āvahatu me, “may it bring welfare to me”. The shining is a statement; the prayer is separate.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

A less daring poet would have put a necklace on Lakshmi. Śaṅkara turns the picture around: her necklace is made of her own glances, and it is worn on Vishnu. Each loving look is a blue gem, and a row of looks becomes a string of jewels. The beauty is not in the ornament but in the affection that makes it. Blue stones lie beside the Kaustubha, and the dark chest and the glowing gem seem to answer one another.

A picture built across the verses

In Verse 2 her repeated glances were a bee moving round a lotus: going, returning, going again. Verse 5 takes those same repeated glances and strings them together. The movement of Verse 2 becomes a lasting ornament. The poet keeps returning to the same few images and lets them grow.

Madhujit and the Kaustubha

Madhujit is a name of Vishnu from the tradition that he slew the demon Madhu. The Kaustubha is the jewel said to have risen at the churning of the ocean, the same churning in which Lakshmi herself arose. The verse places her glances beside the one jewel that came from the same waters.

Kāmapradā bhagavato ’pi

The line says her glance grants wishes even to the Lord. A devotional reading is that Lakshmi stands for grace and compassion, inseparable from Vishnu’s power to sustain the world: the divine pair is complete together. The word kāma can also mean love, which gives a second, gentler sense: a glance that brings delight even to him. This is a reading of the imagery, not a claim that the verse sets out a doctrine.

Grace as an ornament

A necklace is worn to add beauty. Here the ornament is affection itself. We can carry this as a reflection: our titles, possessions and achievements may decorate a life, but kindness, generosity and integrity are what make it beautiful. This is a reflection inspired by the verse, not a line in it.

What do we truly desire?

Kāmapradā means giver of wishes, and it is tempting to read the verse only as a prayer for wealth. But wishes are many: wealth and also security, success and also fulfilment, recognition and also respect, comfort and also peace. The verse invites us to lay our wishes before the Goddess with humility. It does not say that every wish will come exactly as we imagine; we can also pray for the wisdom to see which wishes are worth pursuing.

Kalyāṇa: more than money

The last word is not dhana (wealth) but kalyāṇa: goodness, auspiciousness, welfare. A life can have money and still lack kalyāṇa. The poet asks for the larger thing, well-being that includes right living and good relationships, though the Sanskrit itself says only welfare and auspiciousness.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1ReciteSay the verse slowly, resting on kāmapradā bhagavato ’pi kaṭākṣa-mālā.
  2. Minute 2Picture the sceneSee the Kaustubha jewel shining on Vishnu’s chest, and a garland of Lakshmi’s glances lying beside it like a string of blue gems.
  3. Minute 3Remember kindnessThink of the people whose affection, guidance and kindness have enriched your life.
  4. Minute 4Look at your wishesAsk yourself, “Are my ambitions leading me towards a better life, or only towards more possessions?”
  5. Minute 5Offer a prayer. Say silently“O Mother Lakshmi, may my wishes be guided by wisdom. May my prosperity bring happiness not only to me but to those whose lives touch mine. May my life be filled with kalyāṇa.”

Reflection for today

If Lakshmi’s garland is made of loving glances and not of precious stones, what are the ornaments of my own life? Perhaps the blessings of parents, the affection of family, the trust of colleagues, the ability to help someone in need, the satisfaction of earning honestly. Building wealth is necessary and worthwhile; I can also build a life worthy of the wealth I earn.

The verse turns Lakshmi’s loving glances into a necklace of blue gems shining beside the Kaustubha on Vishnu’s chest, and then asks that this garland of grace bring welfare to the devotee. The thought to carry is simple: the finest ornaments of a life are not always the things we own. Sometimes they are the love we receive, the grace we feel and the goodness we share.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
