The verse

दद्याद्दयानुपवनो द्रविणाम्बुधाराम्
अस्मिन्नकिञ्चनविहङ्गशिशौ विषण्णे।
दुष्कर्मघर्ममपनीय चिराय दूरं
नारायणप्रणयिनीनयनाम्बुवाहः॥

Dadyād dayānupavano draviṇāmbudhārām,
Asminn akiñcana-vihaṅga-śiśau viṣaṇṇe;
Duṣkarma-gharmam apanīya cirāya dūram,
Nārāyaṇa-praṇayinī-nayanāmbuvāhaḥ.

Meaning in short

May the water-bearing cloud that is the eyes of Nārāyaṇa’s beloved, with compassion as its favouring breeze, having driven the heat of evil deeds far away for a long time, give a stream of wealth like falling rain to this distressed young bird who has nothing.

Her eyes are a rain cloud, compassion the breeze that carries it, and the poet a young bird with nothing, waiting.

Key highlights

  • Ambuvāha Cloud: her eyes are a water-bearing cloud
  • Dayā Compassion: the favouring breeze that carries it
  • Akiñcana Fledgling: a young bird with nothing
  • Dhārā Rain: wealth as a stream of rain

Explore the four images

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

AmbuvāhaCloud

Nārāyaṇa-praṇayinī-nayana-ambuvāhaḥ: her eyes are a water-carrying cloud. In the heat of the verse’s picture, a cloud gathering far away is hope that a bird cannot make happen, only wait for.

Pause and reflect: What am I waiting for that I cannot make happen?

DayāCompassion

Dayā-anupavana: compassion is the favouring wind. The cloud holds the water, but it is the wind that brings it near. Resources, too, often reach people through someone’s compassion.

Pause and reflect: Whose compassion has carried something to me?

AkiñcanaFledgling

Akiñcana means one who has not even a little. The poet calls himself a young bird with nothing, in distress. The prayer begins from honest helplessness, not from a list of merits.

Pause and reflect: Can I name honestly what I lack, without shame?

DhārāRain

The wish is for draviṇa-ambu-dhārā, a stream of wealth like rain. Rain falls, feeds and moves on. The verse also asks that the heat of wrong deeds be carried away for a long time.

Pause and reflect: Do I treat what I have as rain that feeds others, or as a hoard?

Notes on each word
दद्यात्Dadyāt

From the root dā, to give. The ending makes it a wish: “may it give, let it give”. It is the main verb of the verse; everything else says who gives, what is given, to whom and what is driven away. Before the next word it is written dadyād.

दयानुपवनःDayā-anupavanaḥ

Dayā is compassion; anu is “along, following”; pavana is wind. An anupavana is a wind that carries something along, a favourable breeze. A cloud goes where the wind sends it; the poet’s picture is a cloud that compassion itself drives towards the one who needs it. The words join as dayānupavanaḥ.

द्रविणाम्बुधाराम्Draviṇa-ambu-dhārām

Draviṇa is wealth, goods, riches; ambu is water; dhārā is a stream or a falling shower. So the shower is of water that is wealth: wealth falling like rain. The final m marks it as the thing given.

अस्मिन्Asmin

The “on” form of idam, “this”. The poet points to himself: on this one, here.

अकिञ्चनविहङ्गशिशौAkiñcana-vihaṅga-śiśau

Kiñcana is “anything”, so akiñcana is one who has not even a little, who has nothing. Vihaṅga is a bird, one who goes through the sky. Śiśu is a child, a young one, a fledgling. The ending means “on”. The devotee is pictured as a nestling with nothing of its own.

विषण्णेViṣaṇṇe

From vi and sad, to sit: to sit sunk down in sorrow. The ending agrees with the fledgling: on the one who sits there, downcast.

दुष्कर्मघर्मम्Duṣkarma-gharmam

Duṣ- is bad; karma is a deed or action, so duṣkarma is a wrong or harmful act. Gharma is heat, the scorching heat of the sun. The compound pictures the results of wrong action as a burning heat. The final m marks it as what is carried away.

अपनीयApanīya

From apa (away) and nī (to lead): to lead away, take off. The ending -ya means “having done”.

चिरायCirāya

Cira is long (in time). It tells us the relief is hoped to last, not to pass at once.

दूरम्Dūram

Used as an adverb: the heat is to be driven far off, not merely eased.

नारायणप्रणयिनीनयनाम्बुवाहःNārāyaṇa-praṇayinī-nayana-ambuvāhaḥ

Nārāyaṇa is a name of Vishnu. Praṇayinī is a beloved, a woman who is loved (from praṇaya, affection). Nayana is the eye. Ambu is water and vāha carrying, so ambuvāha is “carrier of water”, a rain cloud. The poet does not say the eyes are like a cloud: they are the cloud. This is the grammatical subject, the giver. The words join as nayanāmbuvāhaḥ.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, your eyes are clouds heavy with water, and compassion is the wind that carries them. I am a helpless young bird with nothing, sitting in the heat of my own wrong deeds. Let the cloud come to me, take away the heat and send down a rain of wealth.” The poet does not praise himself. He shows himself exactly as he is: in need.

From the text

What the verse actually says

Five pictures stand side by side. Her eyes are the cloud. Compassion is the wind that drives it. Wealth is the rain. The devotee is a young bird with nothing. The results of wrong deeds are a scorching heat. The one wish is dadyāt, “may it give”, and the verse says what is given (a stream of wealth), to whom (the fledgling) and what goes away (the heat). Note that the heat of evil deeds is to be driven away, not denied: the verse names it.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Picture a nestling in the heat of the sun. It cannot fly far or find its own food. It has nothing. Then far away a cloud gathers and a wind begins to carry it nearer. The bird cannot make it rain; it can only wait. Śaṅkara turns this whole scene into a prayer, and the effect is unlike the earlier verses. We have been admiring a goddess of beauty and splendour; now we are with a very small creature waiting for rain. The hymn comes down from the heights of Vaikuṇṭha to a nest.

Why rain?

Rain is generous in a particular way. It falls on the dry field and the green field alike and does not first ask whether the ground has earned it. That is a reflection on the image, not a line in the verse, but it explains why the poet chose it. He longs for grace that gives freely, and he describes wealth not as something kept in a pot but as something that falls, spreads and gives life.

The young bird with nothing

Akiñcana is a strong word: one who has not even a little. The poet calls himself that, and a fledgling too. Sanskrit poets often picture the cātaka, the bird said to drink only raindrops, waiting for the cloud; this verse says only “young bird”, but the feeling is the same. As a reflection: there are moments when our knowledge, money and influence are simply not enough. To admit that is not weakness; it is honesty. We can work hard and plan well and still accept that we cannot control every result.

The heat of evil deeds

Duṣkarma-gharma pictures the results of wrong action as a burning heat. The prayer is not for the deeds to be forgotten or for responsibility to be waved away. A fair reading is that it asks for relief, a cooling, and the strength to live differently. Grace and personal responsibility need not be opposites; we may pray for relief and still set right what we have done.

Wealth as rain, not as a hoard

The verse gives us two ways to see wealth. One is to collect it and guard it. The other is to see it as rain: it falls, it feeds, it moves on. Rain is valuable for what it sustains, the plants, the animals and the people. Taking the picture as a reflection, not a rule in the verse, wealth has a larger purpose when it supports families, creates opportunities and helps those in need, and prosperity does best when it circulates, not when it stands in a closed vessel.

Compassion as the wind

The small phrase dayā-anupavana is easy to miss. The cloud needs the wind to reach us. The poet suggests that Lakshmi’s eyes hold the water, but it is compassion that carries it. This is also true among people: resources alone do not help anyone until kindness moves them to where they are needed.

The thread

How this verse follows the earlier ones

In Verse 3 the poet asked, humbly, for only half a glance. Here the request grows in feeling, not in size: he wants the glance to become a shower. The earlier verses studied the beauty and power of her eyes. This one turns those same eyes into clouds that bring relief to a living creature.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Dayā is compassion: the willingness to see suffering and answer it with kindness. Akiñcana is one who possesses nothing, a word for complete helplessness. Ambuvāha is a water-carrier, a cloud; here a picture of the Goddess’s eyes.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1ReciteSay the last line slowly, nārāyaṇa-praṇayinī-nayanāmbuvāhaḥ, and think of her eyes as clouds filled with compassion.
  2. Minute 2Picture the sceneA young bird waiting in the heat, and dark clouds gathering. Then a gentle rain begins to fall.
  3. Minute 3Look inwardName one area of your life where you feel uncertain, exposed or in need of support.
  4. Minute 4Look outwardAsk yourself, “Is there someone near me who is waiting for a little help, as that bird waits for rain?”
  5. Minute 5Offer a prayer. Say silently“O Mother Lakshmi, take away the heat of my past mistakes. Give me the strength to set my actions right, the wisdom to decide better and the generosity to become a source of relief for others.”

Reflection for today

  • LimitsWhere in my life do I need to accept that I cannot control everything?
  • Past actionsAre there mistakes whose consequences I should address and not merely regret?
  • ReliefCan I be a source of relief for someone who is struggling? Perhaps I cannot always make it rain, but I can often carry an umbrella for someone else.

One honest answer is enough for today.

Here the poet gives up every claim. He is a young bird with nothing, waiting under a hot sky, and he asks that the eyes of Lakshmi become clouds and send rain. The thought to carry is that humility opens the heart to compassion, and that whatever abundance reaches us is best treated as rain: something to receive gratefully and pass on to others who are thirsty.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
