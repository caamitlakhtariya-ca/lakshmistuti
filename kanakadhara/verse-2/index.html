Kanakadhāra Stotram – Verse 2

Mugdhā Muhur – the garland of loving glances

By Amit N. Lakhtariya

Devanāgarī

मुग्धा मुहुर्विदधती वदने मुरारेः
प्रेमत्रपाप्रणिहितानि गतागतानि।
माला दृशोर्मधुकरीव महोत्पले या
सा मे श्रियं दिशतु सागरसम्भवायाः॥

Mugdhā muhur vidadhatī vadane Murāreḥ,
Prema-trapā-praṇihitāni gatāgatāni;
Mālā dṛśor madhukarīva mahotpale yā,
Sā me śriyaṁ diśatu sāgara-sambhavāyāḥ.

Word by wordMeaningDeeper insightsMeditationReflection

Word-by-word meaning

मुग्धाMugdhā

Charming, lovely, innocent, bashful

A description of the Goddess herself.

मुहुः विदधतीMuhuḥ vidadhatī

Again and again casting, directing

Muhuḥ is “again and again”; vidadhatī is making, performing or displaying.

वदने मुरारेःVadane Murāreḥ

Upon the face of Murāri (Vishnu)

Murāri means the foe of Mura, a name of Vishnu, who slew the demon Mura.

प्रेमत्रपाप्रणिहितानिPrema-trapā-praṇihitāni

Glances directed with love and bashfulness

Prema is love; trapā is bashfulness or modesty; praṇihita is directed, placed, fixed upon.

गतागतानिGatāgatāni

Going and coming

Glances that go and return, repeated movements. The word gives movement to the poem.

माला दृशोःMālā dṛśoḥ

A garland of the eyes, of the glances

Mālā is a garland; dṛśoḥ is “of the two eyes”. The poetic centre of the verse: her repeated glances become a garland.

मधुकरी इवMadhukarī iva

Like a female bee

Madhukarī is “she who makes honey”, a female bee.

महोत्पलेMahotpale

On a great lotus

Mahā is great and utpala is a lotus, often a blue lotus.

या साYā sā

She who… may she

Yā (“who”) and sā (“she”) hold the sentence together: the Goddess who is described, and the same Goddess who is asked.

मे श्रियं दिशतुMe śriyaṁ diśatu

May she grant me Śrī

Śrī is prosperity, good fortune, beauty and auspiciousness; diśatu is “may she grant”.

सागरसम्भवायाःSāgara-sambhavāyāḥ

Of her who was born from the ocean

Lakshmi, who emerged at the churning of the ocean.

Full meaning

The lovely, bashful Goddess, born from the ocean, again and again directs towards the face of Murāri glances that come and go, full of love and modesty. Her glances move like a garland, like a female bee around a great lotus. May that Goddess Lakshmi grant me Śrī: prosperity, beauty, good fortune and auspiciousness.

Deeper spiritual insights

In simple words

“O Mother Lakshmi, your eyes are full of love and gentle bashfulness as you look again and again towards Lord Vishnu’s face. Your glances move like bees drawn to a magnificent lotus. O Daughter of the Ocean, bless me with Śrī: with prosperity, grace and auspiciousness.” In Verse 1 we contemplated the power of a single sidelong glance. Verse 2 shows a repeated exchange of glances, full of love and modesty. The first verse presents the beauty of divine grace; the second brings us closer to the emotional intimacy of the Divine Couple.

The poetry hidden in Verse 2

Ādi Śaṅkarācārya could have written, “Lakshmi looks lovingly at Vishnu.” Instead he gives a whole scene of glances that come and go, each carrying a delicate emotion. Imagine Vishnu, calm and magnificent, with the serene beauty of a blue lotus. Lakshmi looks towards His face. Her eyes linger for a moment; then, out of modesty, they turn away; and then they return. Again and again. There is love in the glance, and also trapā, a beautiful reserve. The poet does not need to describe a conversation; their eyes speak for them. A poet sees what ordinary eyes miss.

The emotional genius of the verse

Look at the sequence. The lotus is the image of beauty, freshness and attraction. The bee is drawn to the flower again and again and moves around it. The glance returns again and again, carrying love and bashfulness. The bee’s repeated movement gives us a visual language for the movement of Lakshmi’s eyes. The lotus attracts the bee, and the face of Vishnu draws Lakshmi’s loving attention. The comparison is not merely decorative; it makes the feeling visible.

A subtle distinction from Verse 1

In the first verse the bee image evokes Lakshmi’s graceful movement around Vishnu’s dark, tamāla-like form. In the second, the great lotus becomes the focus: her glances are a garland, like a bee at that lotus. The imagery develops from one verse to the next, but its emotional centre stays the same: the beauty of the Divine Couple and the grace the poet seeks from Lakshmi.

Love and modesty can coexist

Prema-trapā is one of the most tender expressions in the verse. Prema is love and trapā is bashfulness. The Goddess is not portrayed as distant or impersonal: her glances express affection and still keep a graceful reserve. Love does not always need grand declarations; sometimes tenderness is expressed through restraint, attention and quiet presence. This is a poetic picture of divine love, not a rule that everyone must express affection through shyness.

The garland of glances

A garland is made by bringing individual flowers together. Here the poet imagines individual glances as a garland: each glance is a moment of affection, and together they become a continuous expression of love. Repeated acts of attention can form something beautiful. In everyday life a relationship is rarely sustained by one grand gesture; it is sustained by many small ones: listening, remembering, caring, forgiving and showing up.

Why the lotus?

The lotus holds a special place in Indian spiritual and literary traditions. It suggests beauty, purity, freshness and the possibility of flourishing even in muddy water, and it is deeply associated with Lakshmi herself. In this verse, however, the lotus is part of a specific comparison: the bee is drawn to the great lotus just as Lakshmi’s glances turn again and again towards Vishnu’s face. It helps to appreciate both layers, the immediate image and the wider symbolism, without confusing the two.

Why ask for Śrī?

The verse ends with a direct prayer: Sā me śriyaṁ diśatu, “May she grant me Śrī.” It is a beautiful progression from describing the Goddess to addressing her grace. Śrī can mean prosperity, splendour, beauty, good fortune and auspiciousness, so the prayer is broad enough to invite thought about prosperity in its many forms: material well-being, the discernment to use resources wisely, the character to act ethically and the contentment to recognise what is already good. That wider application is a reflection inspired by the verse, not a literal list of meanings in the Sanskrit.

Lakshmi as Sāgarasambhavā

Sāgara-sambhavāyāḥ refers to her who was born from the ocean. In Hindu tradition Lakshmi is associated with the Samudra Manthana, the churning of the cosmic ocean, when she emerged from the ocean of milk and was welcomed by the gods; the account appears in different forms across the Purāṇas. Why does Śaṅkara invoke this identity? He reminds us of her cosmic origin. She is not only a beautiful figure in an intimate scene with Vishnu; she is the revered Goddess born from the cosmic ocean, associated with auspiciousness and prosperity. There is a poetic contrast between the vastness of the ocean and the delicacy of a glance. From a cosmic event the poet brings us to a deeply personal prayer: may she grant me Śrī. The universe is vast, yet devotion makes the prayer intimate.

How to pronounce the key words

Mugdhā: mug-dhaa. Muhuḥ: muhur, “again and again”. Murāreḥ: mu-raa-reh. Prema-trapā: pre-ma-tra-paa. Gatāgatāni: ga-taa-ga-taa-ni. Madhukarī: ma-dhu-ka-ree. Mahotpale: ma-hot-pa-le. Sāgarasambhavāyāḥ: saa-ga-ra-sam-bha-vaa-yaah. One small lesson: when words are joined in a verse their sounds often change slightly. This is called sandhi. We will gradually learn to recognise these joins so that grammar never becomes a barrier to enjoying the poetry.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1, become still: sit comfortably, breathe gently and let the day’s concerns settle. You do not need to empty your mind; simply allow yourself to be present.
  2. Minutes 2–3, recite the verse: read the Sanskrit slowly, resting on prematrapāpraṇihitāni gatāgatāni. Recall the meaning: glances directed with love and bashfulness, moving back and forth.
  3. Minute 4, visualise the scene: imagine a magnificent lotus with a bee gently moving around it. Then picture Lakshmi’s loving glance turning towards Vishnu and returning again. Let the image remind you of tenderness, attention and harmony.
  4. Minute 5, offer your prayer. Say silently: “O Mother Lakshmi, may my attention be loving, my conduct graceful and my understanding of prosperity wise. May I recognise and nurture the goodness already present in my life.”

Reflection for today

Where does my attention return, again and again? The bee returns to the lotus; Lakshmi’s glance returns to Vishnu. Our attention also returns to certain things: sometimes to worry, sometimes to ambition, sometimes to gratitude, sometimes to the people we love. Repeated attention gradually shapes our inner world. What occupies my thoughts most often: fear of losing what I have, desire for what I lack, or gratitude for what I possess? Do the people important to me receive my attention in small, consistent ways? If I seek prosperity, am I also cultivating the qualities that will let me use it wisely? I need not answer every question today; I can choose the one that speaks to me most deeply.

Lakshmi looks towards Vishnu. Her eyes move away and return, carrying both love and modesty, and the poet sees these repeated glances as a garland of bees around a magnificent lotus. Then he makes his prayer: may the Goddess born from the ocean grant me Śrī. Verse 1 taught us to contemplate the power of the divine glance; Verse 2 invites us to contemplate its tenderness, the affection, the repeated attention and the beauty of a relationship expressed without words. What we return to, again and again, becomes an important part of our lives. May we return to wisdom when confused, gratitude when restless, integrity when tempted and compassion when dealing with others. This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
