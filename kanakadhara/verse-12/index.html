The verse

नमोऽस्तु नालीकनिभाननायै
नमोऽस्तु दुग्धोदधिजन्मभूम्यै।
नमोऽस्तु सोमामृतसोदरायै
नमोऽस्तु नारायणवल्लभायै॥

Namo ’stu nālīka-nibhānanāyai
Namo ’stu dugdhodadhi-janma-bhūmyai;
Namo ’stu somāmṛta-sodarāyai
Namo ’stu nārāyaṇa-vallabhāyai.

Meaning in short

Let there be a bow to her whose face is like a lotus. Let there be a bow to her whose birthplace is the ocean of milk. Let there be a bow to her who is the sister of the Moon and of the nectar of immortality. Let there be a bow to her who is the beloved of Nārāyaṇa.

Four bows that tell us who she is: her face, her birth, her family and her beloved.

Key highlights

  • Nālīka Beauty: a face like a lotus, calm and pure
  • Dugdhodadhi Origin: born of the ocean of milk
  • Somāmṛta Kinship: sister of the Moon and the nectar
  • Nārāyaṇa Union: the beloved of Vishnu

Explore the four bows

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

NālīkaBeauty

Nālīka is a lotus, and the first bow is to a face that looks like one. A lotus grows from mud and stays clean, which is why Indian poetry uses it for calm and purity. The poet begins with her face, the part we look at first.

Pause and reflect: What does my face show people when I am under pressure?

DugdhodadhiOrigin

The tradition says Lakshmi rose from the ocean of milk when gods and demons churned it. The poet bows to that birthplace. A reflection: in the old story good things come out of a long, shared effort, not from a single moment.

Pause and reflect: Which patient effort of mine should I not abandon yet?

SomāmṛtaKinship

Sodarā means born of the same womb, a sister. Lakshmi is called sister of the Moon and of the nectar of immortality because all three rose from the same ocean. Prosperity is brought up beside calm and beside what lasts.

Pause and reflect: Does what I am gathering come with calm, or only with worry?

NārāyaṇaUnion

Vallabhā is a beloved, and Nārāyaṇa is Vishnu. The four bows end where Lakshmi’s own story rests: at her husband’s side. Many readers take this as a reminder that wealth sits best beside what is right.

Pause and reflect: Is what I earn kept in the company of what is right?

Notes on each word
नमः अस्तुNamo ’stu

Namaḥ is a bow, a salutation; astu means “let it be”. The two meet as namo ’stu, where the apostrophe marks the lost a of astu. As in Verse 11, the phrase opens every line, but here it comes first and the description follows.

नालीकनिभाननायैNālīka-nibha-ānanāyai

Nālīka is a lotus; nibha means resembling, like; ānana is the face. So she is the one whose face looks like a lotus. The first two parts end in a and the third begins with ā, so they join into -nibhānana-. The ending -āyai means “to”, because namaḥ takes the “to” form.

दुग्धोदधिजन्मभूम्यैDugdha-udadhi-janma-bhūmyai

Dugdha is milk; udadhi is an ocean (literally, a holder of water); janma is birth; bhūmi is ground, place. So dugdhodadhi is the ocean of milk, and the whole word means “to her whose place of birth is the ocean of milk”. Here bhūmi is a feminine word in -i, so its “to” form is -yai, as in śrutyai in the last verse.

सोमामृतसोदरायैSoma-amṛta-sodarāyai

Soma is usually taken here as the Moon; amṛta is the nectar of immortality, from a, not, and mṛta, dead. Sodarā is “of the same womb”: sa, same, and udara, womb or belly; sodarā is a sister and sodara a brother. So she is the sister of the Moon and of the nectar, because in the old story all three rose from the same ocean.

नारायणवल्लभायैNārāyaṇa-vallabhāyai

Nārāyaṇa is a name of Vishnu. Vallabhā is a beloved, a wife, the same word that ended the last verse. The ending means “to”. With this line the four bows reach the one person with whom Lakshmi’s own story is always joined.

Sanskrit structure

How the verse is built. Grammar only, with no interpretation.

Anvaya The sentence in plain order

Unlike Verse 11, the Goddess is not named here. Each line is namo ’stu followed by one description in the “to” form (the dative). The word tasyai, “to her”, is understood but not written, so each line reads: “let there be a bow (to her) who…”. The lines already stand in prose order.

Vibhakti Why the endings are -āyai and -yai

Namaḥ, “a bow”, is joined with the “to” form, the dative: “a bow to…” (Pāṇini 2.3.16). The four descriptions all describe a woman, so each is a feminine word in the dative singular.

Three of them end in -ā (ānanā, sodarā, vallabhā), whose dative singular is -āyai. The second ends in bhūmi, a feminine word in -i, whose dative singular is -yai: bhūmyai, just as śruti gave śrutyai in Verse 11.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, I bow to you whose face is like a lotus. I bow to you who were born in the ocean of milk. I bow to you, sister of the Moon and of the nectar of immortality. I bow to you, beloved of Nārāyaṇa.” Four bows, one in each line.

From the text

What the verse actually says

Each line has the same shape: namo ’stu and then a description of the Goddess in the “to” form. The four descriptions are: she whose face is like a lotus; she whose birthplace is the ocean of milk; she who is the sister of the Moon and the nectar of immortality; she who is the beloved of Nārāyaṇa. The Goddess is not named in the verse. She is known by what is said of her.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Imagine being introduced to someone you have heard about for a long time. First you see her face, and the poet says it is like a lotus: calm, clean and lovely. Then you hear where she comes from: the ocean of milk, a wide white sea in the old story of the churning. Then you meet her family: the Moon, cool and silver, and the nectar of immortality, who rose from the same water. Last, you learn who she belongs to: she is the beloved of Nārāyaṇa.

Notice how the poet widens and then narrows his gaze. He begins with one face, steps back to a whole ocean, looks up to the sky, and then returns to a single person at her husband’s side. The introduction is an image to help us read the verse, in which the four descriptions and four bows are what the poet gives us.

Nālīka, the lotus face

Nālīka is a word for the lotus. In Indian poetry the lotus is the usual picture of calm and cleanliness, since it grows from mud and rises clean above the water. To say that her face is like a lotus is to praise a face that is serene, open and bright. As a reflection: the first thing the poet notices is the way she looks, and he finds in it peace, not riches.

Born of the ocean of milk

The Purāṇas tell of the churning of the ocean (samudra-manthana), when gods and demons churned the ocean of milk and many treasures rose from it, among them the Moon, the nectar of immortality and Lakshmi herself. The lists and the order differ from one text to another. The verse does not tell the story; it only names her birthplace and trusts the listener to know it. As a reflection: in this story good fortune comes out of a long, shared effort, not in a moment.

Sister of the Moon and the nectar

Sodarā means born of the same womb. Here the sisterhood comes from the shared birth: the Moon, the nectar and Lakshmi all rose from the same ocean. The Moon is cool and gentle; the nectar is what does not die; Lakshmi is prosperity. As a reflection: the poet puts prosperity beside calm and beside what lasts. The Ashtalakshmi Stotram describes her the same way: its first stanza calls her the sister of the Moon.

Beloved of Nārāyaṇa

The fourth bow ends at Vishnu, called Nārāyaṇa. Lakshmi is shown at his side in many pictures of the tradition, often seated beside him or pressing his feet. Many readers take this as a reminder that wealth sits best beside what is right and steady. That is a reflection, not a line of the text. The same phrase, vallabhā, closed Verse 11.

The thread

How this verse follows the last

Verse 11 bowed to her as wisdom, delight, power and nourishment: qualities. This verse keeps the same run of salutations but changes the question. It no longer asks what she gives; it asks who she is, and answers with her face, her birth, her family and her beloved.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Nālīka is a lotus. Sodarā is a sister, one born of the same womb. Vallabhā is a beloved.

Say it aloud

How to pronounce the key words

Namo ’stu: na-mo-stu. Nālīka-nibhānanāyai: naa-lee-ka-ni-bhaa-na-naa-yai. Dugdhodadhi-janma-bhūmyai: dug-dho-da-dhi-jan-ma-bhoom-yai. Somāmṛta-sodarāyai: so-maa-mri-ta-so-da-raa-yai. Nārāyaṇa-vallabhāyai: naa-raa-ya-na-val-la-bhaa-yai.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1The lotusPicture a lotus on still water and say silently, nālīka-nibhānanāyai namaḥ. Let your face and shoulders soften.
  2. Minute 2The oceanPicture a wide, pale ocean at dawn. Remember one good thing in your life that came from a long, patient effort.
  3. Minute 3The Moon and the nectarPicture the Moon over the water. Ask for a mind that is cool and unhurried.
  4. Minute 4The belovedSay nārāyaṇa-vallabhāyai namaḥ. Think of one value you want your work to stay beside, and ask that your earning keep its company.
  5. Minute 5A bowBow your head and give thanks for what is already in your life.

Reflection for today

  • FaceWhat do people see in my face when I am under pressure?
  • OriginWhich patient effort am I tempted to abandon?
  • CompanyIs what I am gathering keeping good company, in peace of mind and in right conduct?

One honest answer is enough for today.

In four bows the poet introduces the Goddess to us: her face, her birth, her family and her beloved. The thought to carry is that, in this picture, good fortune is never alone. It rises from a long churning, it is raised beside calm and nectar, and it stands beside what is right.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
