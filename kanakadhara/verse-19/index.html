The verse

॥ श्लोक १९ ॥

दिग्घस्तिभिः कनककुम्भमुखावसृष्ट-
स्वर्वाहिनीविमलचारुजलप्लुताङ्गीम्।
प्रातर्नमामि जगतां जननीमशेष-
लोकाधिनाथगृहिणीममृताब्धिपुत्रीम्॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Dig-ghastibhiḥ kanaka-kumbha-mukhāvasṛṣṭa-
svarvāhinī-vimala-cāru-jala-plutāṅgīm,
Prātar namāmi jagatāṁ jananīm aśeṣa-
lokādhinātha-gṛhiṇīm amṛtābdhi-putrīm.

Meaning in short

At dawn I bow to the mother of the worlds, the wife of the lord of all the worlds, the daughter of the ocean of nectar, whose body is bathed in the pure and lovely water of the heavenly river, poured from the mouths of golden pots by the elephants of the directions.

The elephants of the directions bathe the Goddess at dawn, and the poet bows to the Mother of the worlds.

Key highlights

  • Digghasti Elephants: the guardians of the directions bathe her
  • Kumbha Pots: golden pots pour the heavenly water
  • Prātaḥ Dawn: the poet bows at the start of the day
  • Amṛtābdhi Ocean: she is the daughter of the ocean of nectar

DigghastiElephants

Dik is a direction and hastin an elephant. In the tradition eight great elephants guard the eight directions. Here they serve her. A reflection: this is the largest scale in the hymn, and it is a scene of service, not of display.

Pause and reflect: Who supports me from every direction?

KumbhaPots

Kanaka is gold and kumbha a pot, and the water is poured from their mouths. The water is the river of heaven, pure and lovely, vimala and cāru. A reflection: the verse puts cleansing at the centre of prosperity.

Pause and reflect: What do I want washed away before I begin?

PrātaḥDawn

Prātar means in the morning. The poet chooses the first hour for his bow. A reflection: the first hour is when attention is least spoken for, and giving it first to gratitude changes what the rest of the day is built on.

Pause and reflect: How do I use my first quarter hour?

AmṛtābdhiOcean

Amṛtābdhi-putrī is the daughter of the ocean of nectar. The tradition tells that Lakshmi rose from the ocean when gods and demons churned it. This is the tradition’s story; the verse gives only the phrase.

Pause and reflect: What do I owe to the world that raised me?

Notes on each word
दिग्घस्तिभिःDig-ghastibhiḥ

Dik is a direction; hastin is an elephant, literally “one with a hand”, the trunk. In the tradition the eight directions are guarded by eight great elephants, the diggajas. The consonant h becomes gh after the g, so dik and hasti are written dig-ghasti. The ending -bhiḥ means “by”, in the plural.

कनककुम्भमुखावसृष्टKanaka-kumbha-mukhāvasṛṣṭa

Kanaka is gold. Kumbha is a pot. Mukha is a mouth, an opening. Avasṛṣṭa is “poured down, released”, from ava and the root sṛj, “to let go”. The pots are golden, and the water is poured from their mouths.

स्वर्वाहिनीSvar-vāhinī

Svar is heaven, the sky. Vāhinī is “a flowing one”, a river. So it is the heavenly river, which the tradition identifies with the Gaṅgā in her heavenly form. It is the source of the water in the pots.

विमलचारुजलप्लुताङ्गीम्Vimala-cāru-jala-plutāṅgīm

Vimala is stainless, cāru is lovely, jala is water, pluta is bathed, flooded. Aṅgī means “having limbs”, a body. The final -īm is the “whom” form: it is her, the one whose body is bathed in pure, lovely water.

प्रातःPrātaḥ

The visarga becomes r before the next word, so it appears as prātar. It places the whole scene, and the poet’s bow, at the start of the day.

नमामिNamāmi

From nam, “to bow”, in the first person. It is the main verb: “I bow to her”, and it governs every “whom” form in the verse.

जगताम्Jagatām

Jagat is “that which moves”, the world. The ending -ām is “of”, in the plural: of the worlds.

जननीम्Jananīm

Jananī is a mother, from the root jan, “to be born, to give birth”. The final -m marks “whom”, the object of namāmi.

अशेषलोकाधिनाथगृहिणीम्Aśeṣa-lokādhinātha-gṛhiṇīm

Aśeṣa is “without remainder”, entire. Loka is a world. Adhinātha is an overlord. Gṛhiṇī is the mistress of the house, a wife. The a of loka and the a of adhinātha join into ā. So she is the lady of the house of the lord of all worlds.

अमृताब्धिपुत्रीम्Amṛtābdhi-putrīm

Amṛta is nectar, “the deathless”. Abdhi is an ocean. Putrī is a daughter. The tradition connects this with the churning of the ocean, from which she is said to have risen. The a of amṛta and the a of abdhi join into ā.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“In the morning I bow to the Mother of the worlds. The great elephants of the directions pour pure water from golden pots over her. She is the wife of the lord of all the worlds and the daughter of the ocean of nectar.” One sentence, one picture, one bow.

From the text

What the verse actually says

The verse is one long sentence with a single verb, namāmi, “I bow”. Everything else describes the one he bows to: bathed by the elephants of the directions with pure water from golden pots, mother of the worlds, wife of the lord of all worlds, daughter of the ocean of nectar. The scene of the bath is the traditional picture known as Gajalakṣmī, Lakshmi with elephants.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Imagine a morning ceremony on the largest scale. The eight great elephants of the directions lift golden pots and the water of the sky-river falls on her. The poet does not stand in the picture; he bows to it from outside, at dawn. The grandest image of the hymn is paired with the quietest act: a bow.

Dig-ghastibhiḥ, the elephants of the directions

In the tradition the eight directions are held up by eight great elephants. Here they serve her, which pictures all of space as her household. As a reflection: this is the largest scale of the hymn, and it is a scene of service, not of display.

Svarvāhinī, the water of heaven

The water is pure and lovely, vimala and cāru. The tradition links the heavenly river with the Gaṅgā. As a reflection: the verse puts cleansing at the centre of prosperity, which echoes the white garments of Verse 18.

Prātaḥ, at dawn

The poet chooses the morning for his bow. As a reflection: the first hour is the time when attention is least spoken for; giving it first to gratitude changes what the rest of the day is built on.

Amṛtābdhi-putrī, daughter of the ocean of nectar

The tradition tells that Lakshmi rose from the ocean when gods and demons churned it, and the verse points to this birth with a different name for the ocean, one of nectar, the deathless. This is the tradition’s story and the verse gives only the phrase.

The thread

How this verse follows the last

Verse 18 painted her seated and dressed, and asked for grace. This verse shows her being bathed, with an image on a cosmic scale, and the poet’s bow. The next verse returns to the personal request, and to her glance.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Prātaḥ is the morning. Jananī is the mother. Amṛta is nectar, the deathless.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1DawnSay silently, prātar namāmi. Notice the light of the day coming and how quiet it is.
  2. Minute 2The waterPicture clear water pouring from a golden pot. Say svarvāhinī and feel what it is to be washed clean of last night’s worries.
  3. Minute 3The directionsTurn your attention gently to east, south, west and north, and give thanks to the people in each direction who support your life.
  4. Minute 4The motherSay jananī. Think of the world that feeds and shelters you and give thanks for it.
  5. Minute 5A bowBow your head, say namāmi, and carry the quiet of that bow into the first task of the day.
