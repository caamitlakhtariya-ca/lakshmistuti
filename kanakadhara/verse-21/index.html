The verse

॥ श्लोक २१ ॥

देवि प्रसीद जगदीश्वरि लोकमातः
कल्याणगात्रि कमलेक्षणजीवनाथे।
दारिद्र्यभीतिहृदयं शरणागतं मां
आलोकय प्रतिदिनं सदयैरपाङ्गैः॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Devi prasīda jagadīśvari lokamātaḥ
Kalyāṇagātri kamalekṣaṇa-jīvanāthe;
Dāridrya-bhīti-hṛdayaṁ śaraṇāgataṁ mām
Ālokaya pratidinaṁ sadayair apāṅgaiḥ.

Meaning in short

O Goddess, be gracious! O sovereign of the world, mother of the worlds, one of auspicious form, whose lord of life is the lotus-eyed Vishnu: look upon me every day with compassionate sidelong glances, me who have come for refuge with a heart afraid of poverty.

A prayer from a frightened heart: be gracious, I have come for refuge, look on me kindly every day.

Key highlights

  • Prasīda Grace: be gracious, be pleased with me
  • Dāridrya Fear: a heart afraid of poverty
  • Śaraṇāgatam Refuge: one who has come for shelter
  • Apāṅga Glance: a kind look, every day

Explore the four appeals

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

PrasīdaGrace

Prasīda means “be gracious”. It comes from a root meaning to settle, as water settles and turns clear, and from it comes prasāda, grace. The first request is not for money but for her favour and a calm mind.

Pause and reflect: Where do I need calm more than I need a solution?

DāridryaFear

Dāridrya-bhīti is the fear of poverty. The verse names the fear, not the want. As a reflection, fear of loss can remain even when there is enough, and it can be a worry about money or about what we have not yet learned to trust.

Pause and reflect: What am I really afraid of losing?

ŚaraṇāgatamRefuge

Śaraṇāgata is one who has come to a shelter. It is not giving up effort. As a reflection, it is the humility to do one’s part and to say honestly what is beyond one’s control.

Pause and reflect: What am I carrying alone that I could ask for help with?

ApāṅgaGlance

Apāṅga is the corner of the eye, and a sidelong glance. The poet asks for it every day, with compassion. The same image of the glance runs through the hymn, from Verse 8 to Verse 9, and here it is asked for in its own right.

Pause and reflect: How would my day change if I began it with a kind look?

Notes on each word
देविDevi

Devī is a goddess, and this is the call form. The verse opens by calling her, before any request: she is addressed first and asked second.

प्रसीदPrasīda

Prasīda is a command, second person singular, from pra + the root sad, “to settle”. The idea is of a troubled surface becoming clear and calm, as water does when it settles. From the same root comes prasāda, grace. So the first request is not for money; it is “be gracious to me”.

जगदीश्वरिJagat-īśvari

Jagat is the world, literally “that which moves”; īśvarī is a mistress, a sovereign. The final t of jagat turns into d before the vowel, giving jagadīśvarī, and the long final ī shortens in the call form: jagadīśvari.

लोकमातःLoka-mātaḥ

Loka is a world or realm; mātṛ is a mother, and mātaḥ is its call form. The poet moves from “sovereign” to “mother”: the first word is about her rank, the second about how he dares to approach her.

कल्याणगात्रिKalyāṇa-gātri

Kalyāṇa is auspicious, good, well-being; gātra is a limb or body. So she is “the one whose form is auspicious”. This site follows the reading kalyāṇagātri, as in the text we checked.

कमलेक्षणजीवनाथेKamala-īkṣaṇa-jīvanāthe

Kamala is a lotus; īkṣaṇa is the eye, and kamalekṣaṇa is “lotus-eyed”, a name of Vishnu. Jīvanātha is “lord of one’s life”, a husband. So she is the one whose lord of life is the lotus-eyed Vishnu. The a and ī join into e.

दारिद्र्यभीतिहृदयंDāridrya-bhīti-hṛdayaṁ

Dāridrya is poverty, want; bhīti is fear; hṛdaya is the heart. Together they describe the speaker: “one whose heart is full of the fear of want”. It is the one place in the hymn where he names his fear plainly.

शरणागतंŚaraṇāgataṁ

Śaraṇa is shelter, refuge; āgata is arrived, come. So śaraṇāgata is someone who has come to a refuge. Together with the previous word, it describes mām, “me”: the one with a fearful heart who has come for shelter.

मांMām

Mām is “me”, in the form used for the object of the verb: the one she is asked to look upon. The long phrases before it all describe him.

आलोकयĀlokaya

Ālokaya is a command, from ā + the root lok, “to see”. It means look at, look upon with attention. Prasīda asked for her grace; this word asks for her attention.

प्रतिदिनंPratidinaṁ

Prati means “each” and dina is a day. The request is not for one gift on one day, but for her glance every day.

सदयैरपाङ्गैःSadayair apāṅgaiḥ

Sa is “with” and daya is compassion, so sadaya is compassionate. Apāṅga is the outer corner of the eye, and so a sidelong glance. The ending -aiḥ means “by means of”. The visarga turns into r before the vowel of the next word, giving sadayair apāṅgaiḥ.

Sanskrit structure

How the verse is built. Grammar only, with no interpretation.

Anvaya The sentence in plain order

The verse is one sentence of two requests. The Goddess is called five times, and the speaker is described by a long phrase. In plain order: devi jagadīśvari lokamātaḥ kalyāṇagātri kamalekṣaṇa-jīvanāthe, prasīda; dāridrya-bhīti-hṛdayaṁ śaraṇāgataṁ mām pratidinaṁ sadayair apāṅgair ālokaya.

Vibhakti Why the calls take these endings

The five names are in the vocative, the case of calling. For a feminine word in long -ī (devī, jagadīśvarī, kalyāṇagātrī) the call form shortens the vowel: devi, jagadīśvari, kalyāṇagātri. Mātṛ becomes mātaḥ. A feminine word in -ā (jīvanāthā) is called with -e: jīvanāthe.

Apāṅgaiḥ and sadayaiḥ are in the instrumental plural, the “by means of” case: “with sidelong glances”. Mām is the object. Both prasīda and ālokaya are commands (the imperative), second person singular, because they are addressed to her.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, sovereign of the world, be kind to me. My heart is afraid of poverty, and I have come to you for shelter. Look at me every day with your kind and gentle glance.” It is a short, direct prayer, and it asks for a look, not a sum of money.

From the text

What the verse actually says

There are five calls to the Goddess, two requests and one description of the speaker. The calls are devi, jagadīśvari, lokamātaḥ, kalyāṇagātri and kamalekṣaṇa-jīvanāthe. The requests are prasīda, “be gracious”, and ālokaya, “look upon me”. The speaker is described as one whose heart holds the fear of poverty and who has come for refuge.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Picture someone who has carried a worry for months. He does not make a speech to the Goddess; he says plainly, “I am afraid, and I have come.” Then he asks for something small and daily: a look. The verse is moving because it is so ordinary. There is no grand claim, only a frightened person asking to be seen.

Where this verse stands

In the 22-verse arrangement this is Verse 21, just before the closing verse that speaks of the fruit of reciting the hymn. Not every printed edition carries it; the shorter editions end without it. It reads like a last, personal request added after the formal praise.

Three calls and what they do

Jagadīśvari says she is sovereign over the whole world, so her concern is not limited to one person. Lokamātaḥ says she is a mother, so a child may approach her with fear. Kamalekṣaṇa-jīvanāthe recalls that she is Vishnu’s own, so what she gives is joined with order and preservation. As a reflection: the poet calls her ruler, mother and consort in turn, and asks the mother.

Dāridrya-bhīti, the fear of poverty

The verse does not say “I am poor”; it says “my heart holds the fear of poverty”. The fear is named, not the want. As a reflection, the fear can have several layers: worry about money, a feeling that what we have may not last, and a kind of inner poverty in which there is wealth but little generosity or peace. The last two are readings that grow out of the verse; they are not in the words.

Śaraṇāgata, one who has come for refuge

Śaraṇāgata is the word for someone who has come to a shelter. It does not mean giving up effort. As a reflection: he says what he cannot control and asks for help with it. The Sanskrit tradition values this as humility, the willingness to do one’s part and to admit that not everything is in one’s hands.

The glance, apāṅga

Apāṅga is the corner of the eye, and a glance from there. The same image runs through the hymn: in Verse 8 her eyes are a rain cloud, and in Verse 9 a glance moist with compassion lifts even the wise. Here the poet asks for the glance itself, every day, and does not ask for what it might bring.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Prasāda is grace or serenity. Śaraṇāgati is taking refuge. Apāṅga is a sidelong glance.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1GraceSay silently, devi prasīda. Sit quietly and let your breathing settle, as water does.
  2. Minute 2The fearName one worry about money, work or the future. Say it plainly to yourself, without making it larger or smaller.
  3. Minute 3RefugeSay śaraṇāgataṁ mām. Ask what you can do about the worry today, and what you must leave in other hands.
  4. Minute 4The glanceSay ālokaya pratidinaṁ sadayair apāṅgaiḥ, slowly. Picture a calm, kind look resting on you.
  5. Minute 5ResolveDecide one honest step for today, such as sincere work, a fair earning or a wise use of what you have.

Reflection for today

  • FearWhat am I really afraid of losing, and is it money or something under it?
  • RefugeWhere am I carrying alone what I could ask for help with?
  • EffortWhat one honest step can I take today?
  • UseDoes what I earn feed my family, my work and those who depend on me?

One honest answer is enough for today.

The verse is the hymn’s most personal request: a frightened heart that comes for shelter and asks to be looked at kindly every day. The thought to carry is that the prayer begins with honesty about fear and ends with a daily look of compassion, with sincere effort left in the speaker’s own hands.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
