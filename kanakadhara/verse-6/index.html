The verse

॥ श्लोक ६ ॥

कालाम्बुदालिललितोरसि कैटभारेः
धाराधरे स्फुरति या तडिदङ्गनेव।
मातुः समस्तजगतां महनीयमूर्तिः
भद्राणि मे दिशतु भार्गवनन्दनायाः॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Kālāmbudāli-lalitorasi Kaiṭabhāreḥ,
Dhārādhare sphurati yā taḍid-aṅganeva;
Mātuḥ samasta-jagatāṁ mahanīya-mūrtiḥ,
Bhadrāṇi me diśatu Bhārgava-nandanāyāḥ.

Meaning in short

May the revered form of the Mother of all the worlds, the daughter of Bhṛgu, grant me good things: the form that flashes like a lady of lightning on the rain-bearing cloud that is the chest of Vishnu, the enemy of Kaiṭabha, lovely as a bank of dark clouds.

Lakshmi on Vishnu’s dark chest is a flash of lightning on a rain cloud: the whole figure lit at once.

Key highlights

  • Kālāmbuda Cloud: Vishnu’s chest, dark as a rain cloud
  • Taḍit Lightning: Lakshmi flashing on the dark cloud
  • Bhadra Blessing: the Mother of all worlds, asked for good

Explore the three images

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

KālāmbudaCloud

Vishnu’s chest is pictured as a bank of dark monsoon clouds. The darkness here is not evil: it is the majestic darkness of a cloud that carries rain.

Pause and reflect: Where might something I call dark in my life actually be carrying rain?

TaḍitLightning

Lakshmi’s form flashes on that cloud like lightning, and the lightning shows the cloud’s own beauty. For a moment the whole sky is lit from inside.

Pause and reflect: What brings out the best in the people or work around me?

BhadraBlessing

The third line widens the view to mātuḥ samasta-jagatām, the Mother of all the worlds. The prayer, bhadrāṇi me diśatu, asks for many good things, not one gift.

Pause and reflect: What kinds of good, beyond wealth, am I asking for?

Notes on each word
कालाम्बुदालिललितोरसिKāla-ambuda-āli-lalita-urasi

Kāla is black or dark; ambuda is a cloud, literally “giver of water” (ambu, water, and da, giving); āli is a row or bank; lalita is charming, graceful; uras is the chest, and the ending means “on”. Together: on the chest that is lovely like a bank of dark clouds. The sounds join as kālāmbudāli-lalitorasi.

कैटभारेःKaiṭabhāreḥ

Kaiṭabha is the demon who, in tradition, was slain by Vishnu together with Madhu; ari is an enemy. So Kaiṭabhāri is Vishnu. In Verse 5 he was named by one of the pair, Madhujit; here by the other. The ending marks “of”.

धाराधरेDhārā-dhare

Dhārā is a stream, a falling line of rain; dhara is a bearer. So dhārādhara is “the bearer of streams”, a rain cloud. The ending means “on”: the place where the lightning flashes.

स्फुरतिSphurati

The root sphur is to flash, quiver, glint. Like vibhāti in Verse 5, this is a plain statement of how the form appears, not a wish.

याYā

A feminine “which”. It points forward to the feminine mahanīya-mūrtiḥ, the revered form, in the third line: the form that flashes.

तडिदङ्गनेवTaḍid-aṅganā iva

Taḍit is lightning; aṅganā is a woman, a lady; iva is “like”. The words run together as taḍid-aṅganeva. Lightning is pictured as a lady, so the flash has a figure and a grace. Readers also take it as “a woman bright as lightning”; the picture is the same.

मातुः समस्तजगताम्Mātuḥ samasta-jagatām

Mātṛ is mother; the ending marks “of”. Samasta is all, entire; jagat is world, and jagatām is “of the worlds”. The text writes the last as jagatāṁ when it joins the next word.

महनीयमूर्तिःMahanīya-mūrtiḥ

Mahanīya is “to be honoured”, from mah, to honour and revere. Mūrti is a form, an embodied shape. This is the thing the poet addresses: her revered form, which shines on the cloud.

भद्राणिBhadrāṇi

Bhadra is good, auspicious, fortunate. The plural means “good things”, many kinds of well-being, not one gift.

मे दिशतुMe diśatu

The root diś is to point out, assign, grant. Diśatu is “let her grant, may she bestow”. Me is “to me”. This is the prayer of the verse.

भार्गवनन्दनायाःBhārgava-nandanāyāḥ

Bhārgava means belonging to the sage Bhṛgu. Nandanā is a daughter (the masculine nandana is a son), literally one who gives delight. Tradition calls Lakshmi Bhārgavī, born as the daughter of Bhṛgu. The ending marks “of”.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, on Vishnu’s dark chest, which is as lovely as a monsoon cloud, you shine like a flash of lightning. You are the Mother of all the worlds and worthy of all reverence. May you grant me every kind of good.” The first two lines paint the sky. The third lifts the picture to the whole world. The last line is the prayer.

From the text

What the verse actually says

Two comparisons and one prayer. The chest of Vishnu is like a bank of dark rain clouds. Lakshmi’s form flashes on it like lightning. Then comes the wish: bhadrāṇi me diśatu, “may she grant me good things”. As in Verse 5, the shining is a statement, and only the last line is a request.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Picture a monsoon sky: deep blue, nearly black, heavy with rain. Then, a flash. For a moment the whole cloud is lit from inside, and its darkness shows its beauty. That is how the poet sees Vishnu and Lakshmi. He does not simply say she stands beside him. He turns her into taḍid-aṅganā, lightning pictured as a lady, so that a flash becomes a graceful figure moving across a dark sky. After a necklace of blue gems in Verse 5, the image grows from the chest to the sky.

From the chest to the whole world

Look at what happens in the third line. We have been looking at a close and tender picture of two divine figures. Then the poet says mātuḥ samasta-jagatām, “of the Mother of all the worlds”. The lightning on one cloud becomes the light of the Mother of the universe. The verse moves in one breath from the intimate to the cosmic.

Bhārgava-nandanā

Naming Lakshmi as the daughter of Bhṛgu links her to an old line of sages and gives the prayer a personal warmth. The poet is not addressing an abstract idea of wealth. He is calling to a revered Mother by one of the traditional names that people have called her by.

Light and dark are not enemies

The darkness in this verse is not evil. It is the majestic darkness of a cloud that carries rain. The cloud gives water; the lightning shows its beauty. As a reflection inspired by the image, not a line in the verse, we may notice that a hard season can carry the possibility of renewal. We need not praise suffering to see that a difficult time can sometimes teach patience, resilience and judgement.

More than the goddess of wealth

The verse calls her Mother of all the worlds, which is far larger than “goddess of money”. Tradition speaks of many forms of abundance that she gives: wealth, food, knowledge, courage, family, victory and more. The Aṣṭalakṣmī page sets out the eight forms people most often name. Seen this way, a prayer to Lakshmi is a prayer for a nourished life, not just a full purse.

From beauty to blessing

The earlier verses dwelt on her eyes, her glances and her affection. Here the prayer grows. The poet no longer asks only for a moment of her look; he asks bhadrāṇi, the many good things that her presence brings. It reminds us that prayer is not only asking for things; it can also be making ourselves ready for a life of more goodness.

The thread

How this verse follows Verses 1 to 5

The glance, the bee, the power of the look, the eyes and the necklace of glances: the first five verses stayed close to Lakshmi’s eyes and their light. Verse 6 steps back and lets us see the whole figure against the sky. The poet lifts our gaze, one verse at a time, from eyes to chest to cloud and lightning.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Taḍit is lightning, a flash of light that shows what was hidden. Mūrti is a form or embodied shape, the visible face of something sacred. Bhadra is good and auspicious, the well-being that makes a life flourish and not only a full one.

Say it aloud

How to pronounce the key words

Kālāmbudāli: kaa-laam-bu-daa-li. Lalitorasi: la-li-to-ra-si. Kaiṭabhāreḥ: kai-ta-bhaa-reh. Dhārādhare: dhaa-raa-dha-re. Sphurati: sphu-ra-ti. Taḍid-aṅganā: ta-did-ang-ga-naa. Mahanīya: ma-ha-nee-ya. Mūrtiḥ: moor-tih. Bhadrāṇi: bha-draa-ni. Diśatu: di-sha-tu. Bhārgava: bhaar-ga-va. Nandanāyāḥ: nan-da-naa-yaah.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1ReciteSay the verse slowly, resting on bhadrāṇi me diśatu bhārgava-nandanāyāḥ.
  2. Minute 2Picture the skyA vast, dark monsoon cloud, quiet and heavy with rain. Then a single flash of light that lights up the whole cloud.
  3. Minute 3See the MotherLet the light stand for Lakshmi as the Mother of all worlds, a sign of nourishment, compassion and abundance. You need not force a vision.
  4. Minute 4ReflectThink of one difficult situation in your life and ask what patience, courage or wisdom would help you meet it.
  5. Minute 5Offer a prayer. Say silently“O Mother of all worlds, may your light guide my decisions. May there be prosperity in my home, wisdom in my work, compassion in my heart and auspiciousness in all I undertake.”

Reflection for today

  • ClarityWhere in my life do I most need a flash of clarity?
  • MeasureAm I judging prosperity only by financial growth?
  • RainCan I use what I have gained to nourish and support others, as rain nourishes the earth? Wealth is most meaningful when it creates something good beyond ourselves.

One honest answer is enough for today.

In one image Śaṅkara sets the dark cloud of Vishnu and the flash of Lakshmi side by side, and the sky becomes a picture of divine abundance. Then the picture opens out: she is the Mother of all the worlds, and the poet asks her for every kind of good. The thought to carry is that real prosperity is not only the growth of wealth; it is the arrival of light, wisdom, nourishment and goodness in a life.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
