The verse

श्रुत्यै नमोऽस्तु शुभकर्मफलप्रसूत्यै
रत्यै नमोऽस्तु रमणीयगुणार्णवायै।
शक्त्यै नमोऽस्तु शतपत्रनिकेतनायै
पुष्ट्यै नमोऽस्तु पुरुषोत्तमवल्लभायै॥

Śrutyai namo ’stu śubha-karma-phala-prasūtyai
Ratyai namo ’stu ramaṇīya-guṇārṇavāyai;
Śaktyai namo ’stu śata-patra-niketanāyai
Puṣṭyai namo ’stu puruṣottama-vallabhāyai.

Meaning in short

Let there be a bow to her as Śruti, who brings forth the fruit of good deeds. Let there be a bow to her as Rati, the ocean of lovely qualities. Let there be a bow to her as Śakti, whose dwelling is the hundred-petalled lotus. Let there be a bow to her as Puṣṭi, the beloved of Puruṣottama.

The Goddess is praised as wisdom, delight, power and nourishment: four strands of a flourishing life.

Key highlights

  • Śruti Wisdom: the sacred knowledge that guides good action
  • Rati Delight: love, beauty and lovely qualities
  • Śakti Power: the energy that gives strength and the ability to act
  • Puṣṭi Nourishment: growth, fullness and flourishing

Sketch note

Four forms of one Goddess, drawn as a sketch note: wisdom, delight, power and nourishment together make a flourishing life.

Notes on each word
श्रुत्यैŚrutyai

Śruti comes from śru, to hear: “that which is heard”. It is the traditional name for the Vedas, the revealed scripture handed down by hearing. The ending -yai means “to” and shows that the Goddess herself is being addressed in this form.

नमः अस्तुNamo ’stu

Namaḥ is a bow, a salutation; astu means “let it be”. So the phrase is “let there be a bow to…”. The two words meet as namo ’stu, where the apostrophe marks the lost a of astu. It is repeated four times, once in each line.

शुभकर्मफलप्रसूत्यैŚubha-karma-phala-prasūtyai

Śubha is auspicious, good; karma is action or deed; phala is fruit or result; prasūti is bringing forth, giving birth, a source. So she is the source from which good deeds yield their fruit. The ending means “to”.

रत्यैRatyai

Rati means delight, pleasure, love. It is also the name of the goddess of love, wife of Kāma. Here the sense is usually taken to be delight itself, with Lakshmi as its giver.

रमणीयगुणार्णवायैRamaṇīya-guṇa-arṇavāyai

Ramaṇīya is delightful, charming, from ram, to please; guṇa is a quality, a virtue; arṇava is an ocean. The two a sounds of guṇa and arṇava join into guṇārṇava-. The picture is of virtues as vast and deep as the sea.

शक्त्यैŚaktyai

Śakti is power, energy, ability. In the Goddess tradition it is also the name of the divine power itself. The ending means “to”.

शतपत्रनिकेतनायैŚata-patra-niketanāyai

Śata is a hundred; patra is a petal or leaf; śatapatra is a common Sanskrit name for the lotus. Niketana is a dwelling, a home. So she is the one who lives in the lotus. The number need not be taken literally.

पुष्ट्यैPuṣṭyai

Puṣṭi is nourishment, fullness, thriving. It is the same word asked for in Verse 9. The ending means “to”.

पुरुषोत्तमवल्लभायैPuruṣottama-vallabhāyai

Puruṣa is a person; uttama is the best, the highest; puruṣottama is “the supreme person”, a name of Vishnu. Vallabhā is a beloved, a wife, as in the last verse. The ending means “to”.

Deeper spiritual insights

In simple words

“O Mother, I bow to you as the sacred word that gives good deeds their fruit. I bow to you as delight, an ocean of lovely qualities. I bow to you as power, who lives in the lotus. I bow to you as nourishment, the beloved of Vishnu.” Four bows, one in each line.

From the text

What the verse actually says

Each line has the same shape: a name in the “to” form, namo ’stu, and a description. The four names are Śruti, Rati, Śakti and Puṣṭi. The four descriptions are: she who brings forth the fruit of good deeds; she who is an ocean of lovely qualities; she whose dwelling is the lotus; she who is the beloved of Puruṣottama.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Imagine four offerings placed one after another at the Goddess’s feet. The first is śruti: wisdom, the sacred knowledge that guides us towards what is worth doing. The second is rati: delight, the beauty of love and the lovely qualities that make a life worth living. The third is śakti: power, the unseen energy that gives movement and strength to things. The fourth is puṣṭi: nourishment, the fullness that sustains body, mind and family.

Notice how they follow one another. Wisdom gives direction. Delight gives sweetness. Power gives strength. Nourishment gives continuity. In four bows the poet has gathered a whole picture of a flourishing life. The four offerings are an image to help us read the verse, in which the four names and the four bows are what the poet gives us.

Śruti

Śruti, “what is heard”, is the Veda. The Goddess is honoured as the sacred knowledge itself, and the second part of the line says what that knowledge does: it brings the fruit of good deeds. This reading follows the traditional commentators, for whom the Veda teaches which deeds are good and what they lead to. As a reflection: the first bow is not to gold but to wisdom, and the line suggests that prosperity begins with knowing what is worth doing.

Rati

Rati is delight, and it is also the goddess of love. The poet calls her an ocean of lovely qualities. After wisdom, the second bow is to joy and beauty. As a reflection: a life can be successful and still feel empty, and warmth, kindness and joy are part of what the verse calls Lakshmi.

Śakti and the lotus

Śakti is power, and the line says where that power lives: in the lotus. A later yoga tradition speaks of a thousand-petalled lotus at the crown of the head, but this verse says hundred-petalled, and śatapatra is also simply a common word for a lotus. So the verse need not be read as pointing to that tradition. The central picture is of divine power seated in a lotus.

Puṣṭi

Puṣṭi returns here after Verse 9. There the poet asked for it; here he bows to it. It means nourishment, thriving, being well fed in every sense, and the line ends by calling her the beloved of Puruṣottama, which brings us back to Lakshmi’s own name and place.

From the abstract to the personal

Look at how the four lines are built. The first two descriptions are general: the fruit of good deeds, an ocean of qualities. The last two are about Lakshmi in particular: the lotus is her home and Vishnu is her beloved. As a reflection, the verse moves from ideas to a face.

The thread

How this verse follows the last

Verse 10 bowed to the Goddess of many names. This verse goes on bowing and gives four more of her aspects. After the poet asked and described, the hymn has become a chain of salutations.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Śruti is what is heard, the Veda. Arṇava is an ocean. Puṣṭi is nourishment and thriving.

Say it aloud

How to pronounce the key words

Śrutyai: shrut-yai. Namo ’stu: na-mo-stu. Śubha-karma-phala-prasūtyai: shu-bha-kar-ma-pha-la-pra-soot-yai. Ratyai: rat-yai. Ramaṇīya-guṇārṇavāyai: ra-ma-nee-ya-gu-naar-na-vaa-yai. Śaktyai: shak-tyai. Śata-patra-niketanāyai: sha-ta-pat-ra-ni-ke-ta-naa-yai. Puṣṭyai: push-tyai. Puruṣottama-vallabhāyai: pu-ru-shot-ta-ma-val-la-bhaa-yai.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1WisdomPicture a lotus and say silently, śrutyai namaḥ. Ask that your understanding guide your actions.
  2. Minute 2DelightSay ratyai namaḥ. Think of someone close to you and wish that your dealings with them be warm.
  3. Minute 3PowerSay śaktyai namaḥ. Ask for strength to fulfil your responsibilities.
  4. Minute 4NourishmentSay puṣṭyai namaḥ. Ask that your life, family and work be well nourished.
  5. Minute 5GratitudeBring the four together and give thanks for the wisdom, joy, strength and nourishment already in your life.

Reflection for today

  • WisdomAm I deciding with understanding, or only reacting?
  • WarmthDo people around me feel kindness from the way I act?
  • StrengthAm I using my abilities for something worthwhile?
  • NourishmentIs my family, my office, my community better fed because of what I do?

One honest answer is enough for today.

Four bows give the poet’s picture of a full life: wisdom to choose well, delight to enjoy it, strength to act and nourishment to last. The thought to carry is that prosperity is measured not only by what we gather but by what we grow, in ourselves and in those around us.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
