Kanakadhāra Stotram – Verse 1

Aṅgaṁ Hareḥ – the divine glance of Mahālakṣmī

By Amit N. Lakhtariya

Devanāgarī

अङ्गं हरेः पुलकभूषणमाश्रयन्ती
भृङ्गाङ्गनेव मुकुलाभरणं तमालम्।
अङ्गीकृताखिलविभूतिरपाङ्गलीला
माङ्गल्यदास्तु मम मङ्गलदेवतायाः॥

Aṅgaṁ hareḥ pulaka-bhūṣaṇam āśrayantī,
Bhṛṅgāṅganeva mukulābharaṇaṁ tamālam;
Aṅgīkṛtākhila-vibhūtir apāṅga-līlā,
Māṅgalya-dāstu mama maṅgala-devatāyāḥ.

Word by wordMeaningDeeper insightsMeditationReflection

Word-by-word meaning

अङ्गं हरेःAṅgaṁ hareḥ

The body of Hari (Lord Vishnu)

Aṅga means body or limb; hareḥ is “of Hari”, a name of Vishnu.

पुलकभूषणम्Pulaka-bhūṣaṇam

Having goosebumps as its ornament

Pulaka is the goosebumps that rise from intense emotion; bhūṣaṇa is an ornament. Together: goosebumps that become ornaments.

आश्रयन्तीĀśrayantī

Resting upon, taking refuge in

The glance rests upon the body of the Lord.

भृङ्गाङ्गना इवBhṛṅgāṅganā iva

Like a female bee

Bhṛṅga is a bee and aṅganā a woman or female figure; iva means “like” or “as”.

मुकुलाभरणं तमालम्Mukulābharaṇaṁ tamālam

A tamāla tree adorned with flower buds

Mukula is a flower bud and ābharaṇa an ornament. The tamāla is a tree traditionally associated with a dark complexion.

अङ्गीकृताखिलविभूतिःAṅgīkṛtākhila-vibhūtiḥ

That which has embraced all glories and powers

Aṅgīkṛta is accepted or embraced as one’s own; akhila is all; vibhūti is glory, power, excellence.

अपाङ्गलीलाApāṅga-līlā

The graceful play of a sidelong glance

Apāṅga is the corner of the eye, a sidelong glance; līlā is play or graceful action.

माङ्गल्यदा अस्तु ममMāṅgalyadā astu mama

May it bestow auspiciousness on me

Māṅgalyadā is “giver of auspiciousness”; astu is “may it be”; mama is “for me”.

मङ्गलदेवतायाःMaṅgala-devatāyāḥ

Of the Goddess of auspiciousness

Lakshmi, whose glance is the subject of the prayer.

Full meaning

May the graceful sidelong glance of Goddess Mahalakshmi, which lovingly rests upon the dark, beautiful form of Lord Vishnu and makes His body thrill with divine joy; which moves like a bee among the flower buds adorning a tamāla tree; and which embraces every form of divine glory and prosperity: may that sacred glance of the Goddess of Auspiciousness bless my life with good fortune and well-being.

Deeper spiritual insights

In simple words

“O Mother Mahalakshmi, your glance is filled with such love and grace that even Lord Vishnu’s body thrills with joy when you look upon Him. Your beauty is like a bee moving among flower buds on a dark tree, and you embody every form of divine glory. May your compassionate glance fall upon me and bring auspiciousness into my life.” The central prayer is not simply for gold. It is for the grace of the Goddess who embodies auspiciousness.

The poetry hidden in the first line

Ādi Śaṅkarācārya could have simply written, “Lakshmi looked at Vishnu.” Instead he paints a whole picture. Imagine it: Vishnu stands serene, dark as a monsoon cloud. Lakshmi turns toward Him. She does not stare; she gives only a gentle glance from the corner of her eye. That glance is filled with so much love that His body is covered in goosebumps of divine bliss, sparkling like jewels. A poet sees what ordinary eyes miss.

Goosebumps as ornaments (Pulaka-bhūṣaṇam)

Ordinarily we adorn the body with gold, pearls and precious stones. Here the poet imagines something more beautiful: divine love itself becomes the ornament. Vishnu needs no jewellery to be magnificent; the joy awakened by Lakshmi’s glance adorns Him. The phrase turns an invisible feeling into a visible image. There is a gentle lesson in it for us too: the deepest forms of beauty are not always things we can purchase or possess. Sometimes they are the traces of love, devotion and joy that show in a person.

The bee and the tamāla tree

The second line extends the painting. Imagine a dark tamāla tree, its branches adorned with delicate flower buds, and a bee moving gracefully among them. The poet compares Lakshmi’s glance to this bee and Vishnu’s dark form to the tamāla tree. A bee is drawn to flowers; its movement is delicate and graceful. In the same way her glance brings movement and tenderness to the picture of Vishnu’s serene form. Traditionally Lakshmi and Vishnu are also seen as inseparable, their relationship expressing divine harmony. It helps to keep the layers apart: the bee and the tree are the verse’s own imagery, while inseparability and harmony are spiritual interpretations we draw from it.

The most important word: Apāṅga

Apāṅga is the corner of the eye, a sidelong glance. Why not simply say that Lakshmi looks at Vishnu? Because the sidelong glance adds delicacy, intimacy and grace. In classical Indian aesthetics the eyes are powerful instruments of expression: a glance can carry affection, shyness, compassion, longing or delight without a word. In devotional language a deity’s kaṭākṣa, a gracious glance, is a symbol of divine favour. The devotee is not merely asking to be seen. The devotee longs to receive compassionate attention.

Seek the giver, not merely the gift

The verse ends with a prayer for auspiciousness, not for a specific quantity of gold. The poet turns our attention to the Goddess herself. We may ask for material prosperity, but we can also seek the wisdom, integrity and grace that make prosperity meaningful.

Prosperity is broader than money

The word vibhūti evokes glory, power and excellence. We can think of prosperity as including material resources along with knowledge, good judgement, courage, generosity and contentment. These wider applications are philosophical reflections inspired by the verse, not a literal list contained in it.

Love is itself an adornment

Pulaka-bhūṣaṇam reminds us that emotion changes how we see beauty. A person adorned with jewels may be impressive; a person whose face shines with love, joy and compassion has a different kind of beauty. The verse invites us to appreciate both.

Grace and effort belong together

The prayer asks for divine auspiciousness. In a practical reading, prayer need not replace effort; it can guide it. We may pray for prosperity while working honestly, managing resources prudently, learning continuously and helping others where we can. Devotion gives direction to effort; effort gives practical expression to our intentions.

How to pronounce the key words

Aṅgam: ang-gam. Hareḥ: ha-reh, ending in a soft breath. Pulaka: pu-la-ka. Bhūṣaṇam: bhoo-sha-nam. Bhṛṅgāṅganeva: bhring-gaan-ga-ne-va. Apāṅgalīlā: a-paan-ga-lee-laa. Māṅgalyadāstu: maang-gal-yaa-stu. Do not worry about mastering every sound at once; confidence grows gradually.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1, become still: sit comfortably and breathe naturally. Let your attention settle without forcing the mind to become completely silent.
  2. Minutes 2–3, visualise the poetry: imagine Lakshmi’s gentle glance resting upon Vishnu, and His joy shown as a natural radiance, as though His body were adorned by bliss. Recite the verse slowly, resting on apāṅgalīlā.
  3. Minute 4, offer a prayer. Say silently: “O Mahalakshmi, may I recognise the blessings already present in my life. May I gain the wisdom to use my resources well and the generosity to share what I can.”
  4. Minute 5, carry the prayer into life: choose one action for today. Help someone, share knowledge, express gratitude, or make a thoughtful financial decision.

Reflection for today

What adorns my life: is it merely what I possess, or also my conduct, relationships and inner qualities? Where does my attention rest: am I noticing opportunities for gratitude and goodness, or am I consumed by fear and comparison? What does auspiciousness mean to me: if prosperity came into my life tomorrow, what would make me use it wisely? Do not rush to answer; let one question stay with you through the day.

The first verse joins the grace of Lakshmi, the joy of Vishnu and the beauty of nature in a single poetic moment. The bee, the flower buds, the dark tree, the goosebumps and the sidelong glance are not isolated decorations; they form one living picture of divine beauty and auspiciousness. And the prayer brings that image close to us: may that auspicious glance bless me. Prosperity is worth seeking, but also worth understanding. Wealth can fill a treasury; wisdom helps us use it. Possessions can adorn the body; love and goodness adorn the life. This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
