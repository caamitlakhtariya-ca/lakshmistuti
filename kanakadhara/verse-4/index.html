The verse

॥ श्लोक ४ ॥

आमीलिताक्षमधिगम्य मुदा मुकुन्दम्
आनन्दकन्दमनिमेषमनङ्गतन्त्रम्।
आकेकरस्थितकनीनिकपक्ष्मनेत्रम्
भूत्यै भवेन्मम भुजङ्गशयाङ्गनायाः॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Āmīlitākṣam adhigamya mudā Mukundam,
Ānanda-kandam animeṣam anaṅga-tantram;
Ākekara-sthita-kanīnika-pakṣma-netram,
Bhūtyai bhaven mama bhujaṅga-śayāṅganāyāḥ.

Meaning in short

May the eye of the consort of Vishnu, who rests on the serpent, become a source of prosperity for me: the eye that, having come upon Mukunda with joy, the root of bliss, gazes at him unblinking and ruled by love, half-closed, with its pupil and lashes held in a narrowed, sidelong look.

Not her crown or jewels but the smallest movement of her eye shows how deep the love is. Then comes the wish.

Key highlights

  • Āmīlita Eyes: the half-closed eyes of deep joy
  • Ānanda-kanda Bliss: Mukunda, the root of bliss
  • Animeṣa Attention: an unblinking, loving gaze
  • Bhūti Flourishing: the prayer for prosperity

Explore the four images

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

ĀmīlitaEyes

Āmīlita-akṣam means with half-closed eyes. The lids are lowered in joy and the pupil and lashes are held in a narrowed, sidelong look. The poet shows love through the smallest movement.

Pause and reflect: What small gesture of mine shows what I feel?

Ānanda-kandaBliss

Mukunda is ānanda-kanda, the root of bliss. The poet turns our eyes toward the source of joy, not toward the things we hope will make us happy.

Pause and reflect: Do I wait for a future event to be happy, or look for the source of joy now?

AnimeṣaAttention

Animeṣam is unblinking: she does not look away. We may reflect, beyond the verse, on how rarely we give anything that kind of undivided attention.

Pause and reflect: What would I notice if I gave one thing my full attention today?

BhūtiFlourishing

The wish arrives only in the last line: bhūtyai bhavet mama, may it be for my prosperity. Bhūti is flourishing: income and security, and also sound judgement and healthy relations.

Pause and reflect: What does a flourishing life look like for me?

Notes on each word
आमीलिताक्षम्Āmīlita-akṣam

Mīlita is closed; the prefix ā softens it to “a little, partly”, so āmīlita is half-shut. Akṣa is the eye. The lids are lowered but not closed: the look of someone taking in something dear.

अधिगम्यAdhigamya

From adhi (towards) and gam (to go): to approach, to reach, to come upon. The ending -ya means “having done”, so the arrival is complete before the next thing happens.

मुदाMudā

Mud is delight. The ending means “by, with”, so the coming upon is done with gladness.

मुकुन्दम्Mukundam

A name of Vishnu. Tradition explains it as “giver of mukti” (liberation). The final m marks him as the one the glance goes towards.

आनन्दकन्दम्Ānanda-kandam

Ānanda is bliss; kanda is a bulb or tuber, the underground root from which a plant springs. So Mukunda is not simply happy: he is the source from which bliss grows.

अनिमेषम्Animeṣam

Nimeṣa is a blink, a closing of the eyes; a- negates it. The word sits in the same case as Mukundam, so strictly it goes with him, but translators commonly carry it over to her gaze, which does not turn away. Both readings give the same scene: one steady, absorbed look.

अनङ्गतन्त्रम्Anaṅga-tantram

Anaṅga means “without a body”, the name of Kāma, the god of love, who lives on without form after Shiva burned his body. Tantra here is rule or sway, so the compound reads “governed by Love”. Commentators handle this compound in slightly different ways; the plain sense is the look of someone held by love.

आकेकरस्थितकनीनिकपक्ष्मनेत्रम्Ākekara-sthita-kanīnika-pakṣma-netram

Ākekara means slightly squinting, a narrowed and sidelong glance (from kekara, squinting); some translators render it as “at the corner of the eye”, and the picture is much the same. Sthita is standing, staying; kanīnikā is the pupil; pakṣma is the eyelash; netra is the eye. The long compound is one picture: the eye in which pupil and lashes stay held in that soft, narrowed pose.

भूत्यैBhūtyai

Bhūti comes from bhū, to be or to become: growth, well-being, prosperity. The ending means “for the sake of”.

भवेत् ममBhavet mama

Bhavet is the wishing form of bhū: “may it be, may it become”. Mama is “of me, mine”. Together with bhūtyai: “may it become, for my prosperity”. The two join as bhūtyai bhaven mama.

भुजङ्गशयाङ्गनायाःBhujaṅga-śayāṅganāyāḥ

Bhujaṅga is a serpent; śaya is resting or lying, so “he who rests on the serpent” is Vishnu on Śeṣa. Aṅganā is a woman, a lady, here his consort, Lakshmi. When the two words join, śaya and aṅganā become śayāṅganā. The ending marks “of”.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, I see how you look at Vishnu: with a joy so deep that your eyes are half-closed and your gaze does not move away from him. May that same loving look turn towards me and help my life flourish.” The first three lines paint a picture of love. The last line is the quiet request.

From the text

What the verse actually says

The subject of the verse is her eye. Everything before the last line describes it: the half-closed lids, the steady look, the pupil and lashes in their narrowed pose. Only in the last line does the wish arrive: bhūtyai bhavet mama, “may it be for my prosperity”. The poet never says she looks at him; the glance of her eye is what he hopes will come to him.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Look at what the poet chooses to show. Not Vishnu’s crown, not the splendour of his heaven, not the ornaments she wears. He shows the smallest movement of her eye: the lowered lids, the lashes, the pupil held to one side. Deep affection is often shown this way, not in words but in how we look at someone. Animeṣam, “unblinking”, makes the look feel continuous; mudā, “with joy”, gives it warmth. A tiny physical detail carries a whole world of feeling.

Reading the compounds

The first two lines are a chain of describing words in one case: āmīlitākṣam, ānanda-kandam, animeṣam, anaṅga-tantram. Sanskrit can pile up such words so that they flavour one another without a single verb between them. Translators differ on which word describes her and which describes him, and I have noted the difference in the word list. The picture is the same either way: a gaze that rests, steady and glad, on the source of joy.

The beauty of attention

The verse says she looks at Mukunda with joy and without turning away. What we may reflect on, beyond the verse, is how rarely we give anything that kind of attention. We divide ourselves between work, worry and the next task. This is a reflection inspired by the verse, not a line in it: could our work, or a conversation with someone we love, be better for even five minutes of whole attention?

Bliss as a source, not a prize

Mukunda is ānanda-kanda, the root of bliss. The poet turns our eyes towards the source of joy, not towards the things we hope will make us happy. We often think, “When I earn more or solve this problem, then I will be happy.” The verse suggests a different question: while I work for what I need, can I also notice the sources of peace already in my life? This does not ask us to give up ambition. It asks us not to hang all our happiness on the next achievement.

What bhūti can mean

The verse asks for bhūti: prosperity, flourishing, well-being. For a householder this certainly includes a steady income, the means to support a family and freedom from want. As a wider reflection it may also include sound judgement, healthy relationships, peace of mind and the wisdom to use what we have. These wider meanings are reflections on the word; the Sanskrit itself says only prosperity and flourishing.

Why ask after describing love?

The poet does not first call her a giver of wealth. He first shows her in a moment of loving absorption, and only then asks. One way to read the order is that prosperity is sought in the presence of grace and joy, not as a possession cut off from them. The verse does not promise that reciting it brings wealth. It offers a devotional ideal: ask for well-being while keeping the heart turned towards something deeper than possessions.

The thread

How this verse follows Verses 1 to 3

The first three verses followed the glance: its sidelong approach in Verse 1, its repeated going and returning in Verse 2, its great power in Verse 3. Verse 4 comes close and looks at the eye itself: the lids, the lashes, the pupil, and the love behind them. After the power of the glance, we see its tenderness.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Mudā is joy, doing a thing with a glad heart. Ānanda-kanda is the root of bliss, the source from which joy springs. Bhūti is flourishing: life that grows and prospers.

Say it aloud

How to pronounce the key words

Āmīlitākṣam: aa-mee-li-taak-sham. Adhigamya: a-dhi-gam-ya. Mudā: mu-daa. Mukundam: mu-kun-dam. Ānanda-kandam: aa-nan-da-kan-dam. Animeṣam: a-ni-me-sham. Anaṅga-tantram: a-nang-ga-tan-tram. Ākekara: aa-ke-ka-ra. Kanīnika: ka-nee-ni-ka. Pakṣma: paksh-ma. Bhūtyai: bhoot-yai. Bhujaṅga: bhu-jan-ga. Read slowly and let each line settle.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1ReciteSay the verse gently and without hurry, giving each line its own breath.
  2. Minute 2Picture the sceneImagine Vishnu resting on the great serpent and Lakshmi beside him, looking at him with quiet delight. You need not force a vision; let it come as it will.
  3. Minute 3Look at the eyesRest your attention on her lowered lids and the stillness of her look. Notice how still a loving attention can be.
  4. Minute 4ReflectAsk yourself, “What deserves my whole attention today? What am I pursuing, and why?”
  5. Minute 5Offer a prayer. Say silently“O Mother Lakshmi, may my life flourish. Give me the wisdom to recognise true happiness, the discernment to use prosperity well and the steadiness to stay grateful as I work for my goals.”

Reflection for today

  • AttentionWhen someone I love speaks to me, am I listening, or already busy with something else?
  • AmbitionAm I seeking prosperity as a way of living well, or have I made it the only measure of whether my life is going well?
  • GratitudeWhat is already valuable in my life that I may be overlooking while I chase the next thing?

One honest answer is enough for today.

The verse opens with Lakshmi’s eye resting lovingly on Mukunda, the root of bliss, and ends with a humble prayer that her grace may bring flourishing to the devotee. Its beauty is in the contrast: something large is asked for, well-being and prosperity, by drawing our eyes to something very small, a loving look. Perhaps the lesson is to seek not prosperity alone but a life in which prosperity, joy, attention and gratitude can live together.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
