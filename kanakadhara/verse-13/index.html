The verse

॥ श्लोक १३ ॥

नमोऽस्तु हेमाम्बुजपीठिकायै
नमोऽस्तु भूमण्डलनायिकायै।
नमोऽस्तु देवादिदयापरायै
नमोऽस्तु शार्ङ्गायुधवल्लभायै॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Namo ’stu hemāmbuja-pīṭhikāyai
Namo ’stu bhūmaṇḍala-nāyikāyai;
Namo ’stu devādi-dayāparāyai
Namo ’stu śārṅgāyudha-vallabhāyai.

Meaning in short

Let there be a bow to her whose seat is a golden lotus. Let there be a bow to her who is the mistress of the circle of the earth. Let there be a bow to her who is devoted to compassion for the gods and others. Let there be a bow to her who is the beloved of the bearer of the Śārṅga bow.

Four bows to four forms of the Goddess: the golden lotus seat, the rule of the earth, mercy for all and the beloved of the bowman.

Key highlights

  • Hemāmbuja Seat: she sits on a golden lotus
  • Bhūmaṇḍala Rule: mistress of the whole earth
  • Dayā Mercy: compassion for the gods and all
  • Śārṅgāyudha Beloved: of the bearer of the Śārṅga bow

Explore the four bows

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

HemāmbujaSeat

Hemāmbuja is a golden lotus and pīṭhikā is a seat. The first bow is to the place where she sits. A lotus rises clean from mud, and gold adds wealth. A reflection: she sits on a flower, not on a throne of stone, and the poet notices that first.

Pause and reflect: Where do I sit when I make big decisions, in calm or in hurry?

BhūmaṇḍalaRule

Bhūmaṇḍala is the whole round of the earth and nāyikā is a mistress or leader. The second bow is to her rule over it. A reflection: prosperity here belongs to the world as a whole, and is not only a private matter.

Pause and reflect: What part of the wider world does my work look after?

DayāMercy

Dayā is compassion, and parā says her attention is fixed on it. Devādi means the gods and the rest, so her mercy begins with the highest and spreads to everyone. A reflection: she gives from compassion, not from calculation.

Pause and reflect: Whom could I be kinder to this week?

ŚārṅgāyudhaBeloved

Śārṅga is Vishnu’s bow and vallabhā is a beloved. The fourth bow names Vishnu in his warrior form. Each of Verses 11 to 15 ends with a different name for her beloved, while she stays the same.

Pause and reflect: Is what I earn kept in the company of what is right?

Notes on each word
नमः अस्तुNamo ’stu

The same opening as Verse 11 and Verse 12. Namaḥ is a bow; astu is “let it be”; they join as namo ’stu, where the apostrophe marks the dropped a of astu. It begins every line, and each line then adds one more form of the Goddess.

हेमाम्बुजपीठिकायैHema-ambuja-pīṭhikāyai

Hema is gold; ambuja is “born in water”, a lotus (ambu, water; ja, born). So hemāmbuja is a golden lotus. Pīṭhikā is a seat or pedestal. The whole word means “to her whose seat is a golden lotus”. The two a sounds join into ā, and the ending -āyai means “to”.

भूमण्डलनायिकायैBhū-maṇḍala-nāyikāyai

Bhū is the earth; maṇḍala is a circle, a disc, a realm; bhūmaṇḍala is the whole round of the earth. Nāyikā is a leader, a mistress, from the root nī, “to lead”. So she is the one who leads or rules the whole earth.

देवादिदयापरायैDeva-ādi-dayā-parāyai

Deva is a god; ādi means “and so on, and the rest”, so devādi is the gods and others. Dayā is compassion; parā here means intent on, devoted to. Together: she whose whole attention is on showing mercy to the gods and to all the rest.

शार्ङ्गायुधवल्लभायैŚārṅga-āyudha-vallabhāyai

Śārṅga is the name of Vishnu’s bow; āyudha is a weapon. So śārṅgāyudha is “the one whose weapon is the Śārṅga bow”, a name of Vishnu. Vallabhā is a beloved, the same word that ended Verse 11 and Verse 12. Here Vishnu appears as the bowman.

Sanskrit structure

How the verse is built. Grammar only, with no interpretation.

Anvaya The sentence in plain order

As in Verse 12, the Goddess is not named. Each line is namo ’stu followed by one description in the “to” form (the dative). The word tasyai, “to her”, is understood but not written. The lines already stand in prose order.

The patternThe description in the “to” form

Vibhakti Why all four end in -āyai

Namaḥ, “a bow”, is joined with the “to” form, the dative: “a bow to…” (Pāṇini 2.3.16). All four descriptions describe a woman, so each is a feminine word in the dative singular.

Every one of them ends in -ā (pīṭhikā, nāyikā, parā, vallabhā), whose dative singular is -āyai. In a compound the ending goes on the last part only.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, I bow to you whose seat is a golden lotus. I bow to you who rule the whole earth. I bow to you whose mercy is for the gods and for everyone else. I bow to you, the beloved of Vishnu, the bowman.” Four bows, one in each line.

From the text

What the verse actually says

Every line has the same shape as Verse 12: namo ’stu and then a description of the Goddess in the “to” form. The four descriptions are: she whose seat is a golden lotus; she who is mistress of the circle of the earth; she who is intent on compassion for the gods and others; and she who is the beloved of the bearer of the Śārṅga bow. She is not named; she is known by what she is.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Think of a visitor shown around a great house. First the room where she sits: a golden lotus. Then the extent of her care: the whole round of the earth. Then the mood she keeps there: mercy. And at last the person beside her: Vishnu, bow in hand. Each line widens or deepens what we know of her.

Hemāmbuja-pīṭhikā, the golden lotus seat

The lotus is the Goddess’s usual seat in the tradition, and here it is golden. A lotus rises clean from mud, so in Indian poetry it stands for something beautiful that does not carry the mark of where it grew. Gold adds the sense of wealth. As a reflection: she does not sit on a throne of stone but on a flower, and the poet notices that first.

Bhūmaṇḍala-nāyikā, mistress of the earth

Maṇḍala is a circle, so the phrase pictures the earth as a round whole with her at its head. The tradition links Lakshmi with the earth’s abundance, crops, wealth and well-being. As a reflection: the verse praises her rule without saying how she rules; it asks us to see prosperity as part of how the whole world is sustained, not only as something private.

Devādi-dayāparā, mercy for gods and others

The word devādi means the gods and the rest, so her mercy begins with those highest in the old picture and then spreads outward to everyone. The word parā says her attention is fixed on it. As a reflection: even the gods are said to need her favour, and she gives it from compassion, not from calculation.

Śārṅgāyudha-vallabhā, the beloved of the bowman

The Śārṅga is Vishnu’s bow, said in tradition to have been made of horn. The poet picks the warrior name of Vishnu here. Note the pattern of the last words of the verses: Puruṣottama in Verse 11, Nārāyaṇa in Verse 12, Śārṅgāyudha here. Her husband gets a new name each time, while she stays the same. That is a reflection, not a line of the text.

Why Śaṅkara may have arranged the bows in this order

This is a reflection; Śaṅkara does not explain his order. The four bows move from her appearance (a golden lotus seat), to her authority (mistress of the circle of the earth), to her character (devoted to compassion), and last to her relationship (beloved of the bowman, Vishnu). Notice that after speaking of her rule, Śaṅkara turns at once to her compassion, as if to say that authority is complete only when it is joined to mercy. That is one reader’s way of seeing the pattern.

Four principles from Śaṅkara’s four images

Also a reflection on the imagery, not a literal translation. The golden lotus: let prosperity rest on purity of conduct. The mistress of the earth: greater resources bring greater responsibility. The line on compassion: measure success also by what becomes better because you are there. The beloved of Vishnu: in the Vaiṣṇava tradition Lakṣmī and Nārāyaṇa are inseparable, so keep wealth joined to dharma, that is to right conduct and responsibility.

The thread

How this verse follows the last

Verse 12 told us who she is: her face, her birth, her family and her beloved. This verse keeps the same form and adds where she sits, what she governs and how she feels toward those who ask. The hymn is building a portrait, one bow at a time.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Pīṭhikā is a seat. Nāyikā is a mistress, a leader. Dayā is compassion.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1The seatPicture a golden lotus on still water and say silently, hemāmbuja-pīṭhikāyai namaḥ. Let your shoulders drop.
  2. Minute 2The earthPicture the whole round earth and say bhūmaṇḍala-nāyikāyai namaḥ. Give thanks for one thing the earth gave you today.
  3. Minute 3MercySay devādi-dayāparāyai namaḥ. Think of one person you can treat with a little more patience today.
  4. Minute 4The belovedSay śārṅgāyudha-vallabhāyai namaḥ. Ask that your work stay beside what is steady and right.
  5. Minute 5A bowBow your head and give thanks for what you already have.
