The verse

॥ श्लोक १३ ॥

नमोऽस्तु हेमाम्बुजपीठिकायै
नमोऽस्तु भूमण्डलनायिकायै।
नमोऽस्तु देवादिदयापरायै
नमोऽस्तु शार्ङ्गायुधवल्लभायै॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Namo ’stu hemāmbuja-pīṭhikāyai
Namo ’stu bhūmaṇḍala-nāyikāyai;
Namo ’stu devādi-dayāparāyai
Namo ’stu śārṅgāyudha-vallabhāyai.

Meaning in short

Salutations to the Goddess who is seated upon a golden lotus. Salutations to the sovereign Lady of the entire earthly world. Salutations to her whose compassion extends to the gods and all beings. Salutations to the beloved consort of Lord Vishnu, the wielder of the divine bow Śārṅga.

Four bows to four forms of the Goddess: the golden lotus seat, the rule of the earth, mercy for all and the beloved of the bowman.

Key highlights

  • Hemāmbuja Seat: she sits on a golden lotus
  • Bhūmaṇḍala Rule: mistress of the whole earth
  • Dayā Mercy: compassion for the gods and all
  • Śārṅgāyudha Beloved: of the bearer of the Śārṅga bow

Explore the four bows

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

HemāmbujaSeat

Hemāmbuja is a golden lotus and pīṭhikā is a seat. The first bow is to the place where she sits. A lotus rises clean from mud, and gold adds wealth. A reflection: she sits on a flower, not on a throne of stone, and the poet notices that first.

Pause and reflect: Where do I sit when I make big decisions, in calm or in hurry?

BhūmaṇḍalaRule

Bhūmaṇḍala is the whole round of the earth and nāyikā is a mistress or leader. The second bow is to her rule over it. A reflection: prosperity here belongs to the world as a whole, and is not only a private matter.

Pause and reflect: What part of the wider world does my work look after?

DayāMercy

Dayā is compassion, and parā says her attention is fixed on it. Devādi means the gods and the rest, so her mercy begins with the highest and spreads to everyone. A reflection: she gives from compassion, not from calculation.

Pause and reflect: Whom could I be kinder to this week?

ŚārṅgāyudhaBeloved

Śārṅga is Vishnu’s bow and vallabhā is a beloved. The fourth bow names Vishnu in his warrior form. Each of Verses 11 to 15 ends with a different name for her beloved, while she stays the same.

Pause and reflect: Is what I earn kept in the company of what is right?

Notes on each word
नमः अस्तुNamo ’stu

Namaḥ is a salutation and astu is “let it be”. Through sandhi namaḥ astu becomes namo ’stu, where the apostrophe marks the dropped a. It begins every line of the verse, as it did in Verse 11 and Verse 12.

हेमHema

The metal of wealth and splendour. Here it makes the lotus golden.

अम्बुजAmbuja

Ambu is water and ja is “born”. A lotus is “water-born”.

पीठिकाPīṭhikā

A seat on which a deity or a ruler sits.

हेमाम्बुजपीठिकायैHemāmbuja-pīṭhikāyai

The two a sounds of hema and ambuja join into ā. The ending -āyai is the feminine “to” form, so the whole word is a salutation addressed to the Goddess.

भूमण्डलBhū-maṇḍala

Bhū is the earth and maṇḍala is a circle, a disc or a realm. Together they give the whole round of the earth.

नायिकाNāyikā

From the root nī, “to lead”. A nāyikā is a woman who leads, a mistress.

भूमण्डलनायिकायैBhūmaṇḍala-nāyikāyai

Again the ending -āyai: “to” the Goddess who presides over the earthly sphere.

देवादिDeva-ādi

Deva is a god and ādi is “and so on, beginning with”. So her compassion begins with the gods and goes out to all the rest.

दयाDayā

Tender care for another’s suffering or need.

पराParā

On its own parā can mean supreme or foremost. At the end of a compound, as here, it also has the sense “intent on, wholly devoted to”: she whose first concern is compassion.

देवादिदयापरायैDevādi-dayā-parāyai

The visible chain is deva + ādi + dayā + parā, with a + ā joining into ā in devādi. A salutation to her whose attention rests on mercy.

शार्ङ्गŚārṅga

In the tradition the bow of Vishnu is called Śārṅga.

आयुधĀyudha

Anything used to fight with. Śārṅgāyudha is therefore “he whose weapon is the Śārṅga bow”, a name of Vishnu.

वल्लभाVallabhā

The same word that ended Verse 11 and Verse 12. Here the beloved is of Vishnu the bowman.

शार्ङ्गायुधवल्लभायैŚārṅgāyudha-vallabhāyai

The last bow names her through her beloved. The a of śārṅga and the ā of āyudha join into ā.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

Before we begin

We now move from Verse 12, which praised Lakshmi’s divine origin and her relationship with Nārāyaṇa, to a verse that presents her in four powerful dimensions: divine splendour, universal sovereignty, boundless compassion and inseparable union with Vishnu.

“Salutations to the Goddess who is seated upon a golden lotus. Salutations to the sovereign Lady of the entire earthly world. Salutations to her whose compassion extends to the gods and all beings. Salutations to the beloved consort of Lord Vishnu, the wielder of the divine bow Śārṅga.”

1The simple translation

This is the straightforward meaning of the four lines. Notice the repeated feminine ending -āyai: each expression is addressed to the Goddess, “salutations to her who is…”.

2The deeper meaning of the four images

First image – Hemāmbuja-pīṭhikā: the golden lotus throne

The lotus is a familiar symbol of purity: it grows in water and mud, yet its beauty is not diminished by the environment in which it grows.

The golden lotus adds another dimension: splendour, auspiciousness and divine abundance.

Lakshmi is not merely surrounded by gold; she is seated upon a lotus made golden. The image brings together purity and prosperity.

Contemplation: Wealth is most auspicious when it rests upon purity of conduct.

Second image – Bhūmaṇḍala-nāyikā: sovereign of the world

Here Śaṅkara moves beyond the image of a beautiful Goddess seated on a lotus. She is the presiding Lady of the entire earthly sphere.

Think about the significance: Lakshmi is not being praised merely as the giver of individual wealth, jewellery or gold. Her presence is associated with the prosperity and well-being of the world itself.

This is a much wider understanding of Lakshmi: agriculture, food, resources, trade, learning, institutions, relationships and the conditions that allow life to flourish.

Third image – Devādi-dayā-parā: the embodiment of compassion

This is perhaps the most important expression in the verse. Deva-ādi is the gods and others, dayā is compassion and parā is supreme, foremost. She is praised as one whose compassion extends to the gods and others.

Notice the sequence: the poet has just described her as the sovereign of the world. But immediately after describing her authority, he describes her compassion. That is a profound pairing.

Contemplation: Power without compassion can become domination. Power guided by compassion becomes protection.

Fourth image – Śārṅgāyudha-vallabhā: the beloved of Vishnu

Śārṅga is the divine bow of Lord Vishnu. Āyudha means weapon, and vallabhā means beloved or consort. The poet identifies Lakshmi through her intimate relationship with Vishnu.

Vishnu represents the sustaining and protecting principle of the universe. Lakshmi, as his inseparable divine consort, represents auspiciousness, abundance and grace.

The image is not simply about a divine marriage. It expresses the inseparability of preservation and prosperity, protection and grace.

3Why has Śaṅkara arranged these four descriptions in this order?

OrderDescriptionCentral idea
1Golden lotus seatDivine purity and splendour
2Sovereign of the worldUniversal authority
3Supreme compassionKindness and protection
4Beloved of VishnuDivine unity and preservation

This is a reading of the sequence; the verse itself does not explain its order. There is a movement from the Goddess’s appearance, to her authority, to her character, and finally to her divine relationship.

And there is something worth noticing: when the poet speaks of her sovereignty, he does not stop there. He immediately reminds us of her compassion. It is almost as if he is teaching us what true greatness should look like. A person may possess wealth, authority and influence. But without compassion, these qualities remain incomplete.

4The philosophical interpretation: Lakshmi beyond money

1
Golden lotus: earn, but remain pure.

Let prosperity come into your life, but do not allow it to corrupt your values.

2
Sovereign of the world: understand responsibility.

The greater your resources and authority, the greater your responsibility towards others.

3
Compassion: let your success benefit others.

The measure of prosperity is not only what you accumulate, but also what becomes better because you are there.

4
Beloved of Vishnu: keep wealth connected with dharma.

In the traditional Vaishnava understanding, Lakshmi and Narayana are inseparable. Prosperity should remain connected with preservation, righteousness and responsibility.

This is a contemplative interpretation of the imagery, rather than a literal translation of the Sanskrit.

5A poetic observation: why four times namo ’stu?

Namaḥ is salutation and astu is “let it be”. The repetition is deliberate. Each line offers a fresh salutation, as though the poet refuses to let even one dimension of the Goddess pass without reverence. It is also a poetic device called anaphora: repeating the same expression at the beginning of successive lines.

But there is a devotional dimension too. The poet does not merely say, “Lakshmi is beautiful, powerful and compassionate.” He bows separately to each quality. That is the beauty of stuti: praise becomes an act of worship.

Three Sanskrit words to keep

  • Pīṭhikā – a seat, a pedestal
  • Nāyikā – a mistress, a leader
  • Dayā – compassion

A question to carry into Verse 14

Verse 13 describes Lakshmi as the sovereign of the world and the beloved of Vishnu. Verse 14 takes us closer to her personal and divine identity: as Bhṛgu’s daughter, as the one who resides on Vishnu’s chest, and as the beloved of Dāmodara.

The next verse will help us explore why Lakshmi is described both as a universal cosmic power and as the intimate companion of Vishnu.

Practical meditation

  1. Pause after each line and contemplate it. First line, namo ’stu hemāmbuja-pīṭhikāyai: “May purity and auspicious prosperity find a seat in my life.”
  2. Second line, namo ’stu bhūmaṇḍala-nāyikāyai: “May I understand that resources and authority come with responsibility.”
  3. Third line, namo ’stu devādi-dayā-parāyai: “May compassion become the foundation of my thoughts and actions.”
  4. Fourth line, namo ’stu śārṅgāyudha-vallabhāyai: “May my prosperity remain aligned with dharma and the sustaining principle of life.”

True Lakshmi is not merely the presence of gold in one’s home; it is the presence of purity, responsibility, compassion and dharma in one’s life.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
