The verse

॥ श्लोक १३ ॥

सम्पत्कराणि सकलेन्द्रियनन्दनानि
साम्राज्यदानविभवानि सरोरुहाक्षि।
त्वद्वन्दनानि दुरिताहरणोद्यतानि
मामेव मातरनिशं कलयन्तु मान्ये॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Sampat-karāṇi sakalendriya-nandanāni
Sāmrājya-dāna-vibhavāni saroruhākṣi;
Tvad-vandanāni duritāharaṇodyatāni
Mām eva mātar aniśaṁ kalayantu mānye.

Meaning in short

O lotus-eyed Mother, worthy of honour! May my salutations to you, which bring prosperity, delight all the senses, have the power to bestow sovereignty and are always ready to remove misfortune, attend me alone, without pause.

Four fruits of homage: prosperity, delight, sovereignty and release from misfortune, and a prayer that the homage never stops.

Key highlights

  • Sampat Prosperity: homage that brings good fortune
  • Nandana Delight: it gladdens all the senses
  • Sāmrājya Sovereignty: it can bestow rule and mastery
  • Durita Release: it is ready to remove misfortune

Explore the four fruits

Tap a card to open its meaning and a question to carry with you.

SampatProsperity

Sampat is wealth, good fortune, flourishing. The verse says the poet’s own salutations are sampat-kara, “makers of prosperity”. Notice that it is the homage that is said to bring it. A reflection: prosperity here is wider than money; it includes work, health and steadiness.

Pause and reflect: What already supports my life that I have stopped noticing?

NandanaDelight

Nandana is delighting, and sakala-indriya is all the senses. The poet claims that homage does not dull the senses but gladdens them. A reflection: a thankful mind can notice more beauty, a sound, a taste, a sight, and enjoy it with less hurry.

Pause and reflect: When did I last enjoy something simple without hurry?

SāmrājyaSovereignty

Sāmrājya is sovereignty, rule over a great realm, and dāna is giving. The salutations are said to have the might to bestow it. As a reflection, many readers take sovereignty also as mastery of oneself: of habit, temper and spending.

Pause and reflect: Which one habit would I like to govern first?

DuritaRelease

Durita is what has gone wrong, misfortune or fault, and udyata means ready or intent. The homage is “ready to remove” it. A reflection: prayer does not take the place of action, but it can steady us enough to put right what we can.

Pause and reflect: Which fault or worry will I take one step to set right this week?

Notes on each word
सम्पत्कराणिSampat-karāṇi

Sampat is wealth, good fortune, flourishing; kara, from the root kṛ, “to make”, means making or doing. So sampat-kara is “maker of prosperity”. The ending -āṇi is neuter plural, which tells us the word describes the salutations (vandanāni), not the Goddess. She is only addressed later in the verse.

सकलेन्द्रियनन्दनानिSakala-indriya-nandanāni

Sakala is all, whole; indriya is a sense, such as sight or hearing; nandana is delighting, gladdening. The a of sakala and the i of indriya join into e, giving sakalendriya. The poet says that homage does not dull the senses; it gladdens them.

साम्राज्यदानविभवानिSāmrājya-dāna-vibhavāni

Sāmrājya is sovereignty, rule over a great realm; dāna is giving; vibhava is might, power, splendour. Together: salutations whose might lies in giving sovereignty. A few printed editions read dhana, “wealth”, in place of dāna; this site follows dāna, the reading in the text we checked.

सरोरुहाक्षिSaroruha-akṣi

Saroruha is a lotus, literally “growing in a lake” (saras, lake; ruh, to grow); akṣi is the eye. The two a sounds join into ā. This is a call to the Goddess. In Verse 12 her face was like a lotus; here her eyes are.

त्वद्वन्दनानिTvad-vandanāni

Tvad is the form of “you” used inside compounds; vandana is a salutation, an act of honouring. Vandanāni is the plural, and it is the subject of the whole verse: all four descriptions and the verb belong to it. So the verse is about the power of the poet’s own acts of homage.

दुरिताहरणोद्यतानिDurita-āharaṇa-udyatāni

Durita is what has gone wrong: misfortune, fault, sin. Āharaṇa is taking away, removal. Udyata means raised, ready, intent upon. So the salutations are “intent on taking away what has gone wrong”. Some editions read duritoddharaṇa, “lifting out of misfortune”; the sense is close.

माम् एवMām eva

Mām is “me”, in the form used for the object; eva means only, alone, indeed. In writing they run together: मामेव. The poet is speaking for himself, as he did in Verse 8 when he called himself a young bird with nothing.

मातर्Mātar

Mātṛ is mother, and this is the call form. In the Devanagari it runs into the next word: मातरनिशं. The call comes from the poet’s own affection: after calling her lotus-eyed, he now calls her simply Mother.

अनिशम्Aniśam

Aniśam is an adverb meaning without pause, continually. It goes with the verb kalayantu. It is the word that turns the last line into a prayer for steadiness, not for a single event.

कलयन्तुKalayantu

Kalayantu is a command in the “let it be” mood (the imperative), third person plural: “let them…”. Its subject is vandanāni. The root kal has several shades: to bring, to bear, to hold, to attend. Translators render it in different ways, for example “may they always remain with me”; this site keeps the wider sense, “may they attend me”.

मान्येMānye

Mānyā means to be honoured, worthy of respect, from the root mān, “to honour”. The call form for a woman is mānye. The verse ends on a note of respect: the poet has asked for much and finishes by calling her worthy of honour.

Sanskrit structure

How the verse is built. Grammar only, with no interpretation.

Anvaya The sentence in plain order

The verse is one sentence. Its subject is vandanāni, “salutations”; the four descriptions all agree with it. The Goddess is only called, in the “calling” case (the vocative). In plain order the sentence reads: saroruhākṣi mātar mānye, tvad-vandanāni sampat-karāṇi sakalendriya-nandanāni sāmrājya-dāna-vibhavāni duritāharaṇodyatāni mām eva aniśaṁ kalayantu.

Vibhakti Why the four descriptions end in -āni

Vandana, “salutation”, is a neuter word in -a. Its plural in the first case, the nominative, is vandanāni. Anything that describes it must agree in case, number and gender, so each of the four descriptions is also neuter plural in -āni. In a compound the ending goes only on the last part.

The three calls to the Goddess are in the vocative, the case of calling. Saroruhākṣi is the call form of saroruhākṣī, mātar is the call form of mātṛ, “mother”, and mānye is the call form of mānyā. The verb kalayantu is in the “let it be” mood (the imperative), third person plural, because its subject, vandanāni, is plural.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O lotus-eyed Mother, worthy of honour: my salutations to you bring prosperity, gladden the senses, can bestow sovereignty and are always ready to remove misfortune. Let them be with me, and with me alone, without a break.” It is one sentence, and its subject is the poet’s own act of bowing.

From the text

What the verse actually says

The Goddess is not described here; the bowing is. Four descriptions, all in the same form, qualify vandanāni, “salutations”: they bring prosperity, they delight all the senses, they have the power to bestow sovereignty and they are ready to remove misfortune. Then comes the prayer: mām eva … aniśaṁ kalayantu, “let them attend me alone, without pause”.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

After four verses of bows, the poet stops to say what a bow is worth. Imagine a person who has been walking to a temple every morning and now says, quietly, what those walks have given him: a little more to live on, a little more joy in ordinary things, a little more command of himself, a little less weight of old mistakes. Then he asks only that the walking never stop.

Sampat, the first fruit

Sampat is wealth, but also good fortune and flourishing in general. The verse says the salutations are sampat-kara, makers of it. As a reflection: prosperity here is wider than money. It includes work, health, family and the steadiness to use them well. The hymn’s own request for wealth in Verse 8 is the more direct one; this verse says what the habit of homage can do.

The delight of the senses

Sakala-indriya-nandana means gladdening all the senses. Many people picture devotion as turning away from the senses; this line says the opposite. As a reflection: a mind that is thankful tends to notice more of what is beautiful, a sound, a taste, a sight, and enjoys it with less hurry.

Sovereignty

Sāmrājya is rule over a large realm. In the old picture it is the highest worldly power. Many readers take it also in an inner sense, as mastery of oneself: of habit, temper and spending. That is a reflection, not a line of the text; the verse itself says only that the salutations have the might to bestow sovereignty.

Release from misfortune

Durita is what has gone wrong. The salutations are called udyata, ready or intent, to remove it. As a reflection: prayer does not take the place of action, but it can steady a person enough to put right what can be put right.

Kalayantu: stay or attend?

The last verb has more than one shade. Some translators write “may they always remain with me”; others “may they ever attend me”. All of them keep what the line plainly says: it is a prayer, it is for himself, and it is without pause. This site keeps the wider “attend me”.

The thread

How this verse follows the last

Verse 12 gave four bows that told us who she is. This verse turns from her to the bows themselves and asks what they do. The question moves from “who is she?” to “what does honouring her bring me?”, and the poet answers in four fruits.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Vandana is a salutation. Sampat is prosperity. Durita is misfortune or fault.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1ProsperitySay silently, sampat-karāṇi. Name one thing in your life that already supports you, and give thanks for it.
  2. Minute 2DelightSay sakalendriya-nandanāni. Notice one sound, one smell and one sight around you, and let them be enough for a moment.
  3. Minute 3SovereigntySay sāmrājya-dāna-vibhavāni. Ask where you need more command of your own habits, temper or spending.
  4. Minute 4ReleaseSay duritāharaṇodyatāni. Name one mistake or worry you are carrying, and decide one small step to set it right.
  5. Minute 5SteadinessSay mām eva mātar aniśaṁ kalayantu mānye, slowly, and ask that this attention stay with you through the day.

One honest answer is enough for today.

The poet names four fruits of homage: prosperity, delight, mastery and relief from misfortune. Then he asks for something else: that the homage itself should not stop. The thought to carry is that the prayer asks for steadiness before it asks for anything more.
