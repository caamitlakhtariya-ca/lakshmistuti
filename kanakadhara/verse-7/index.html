The verse

प्राप्तं पदं प्रथमतः खलु यत्प्रभावान्
माङ्गल्यभाजि मधुमाथिनि मन्मथेन।
मय्यापतेत्तदिह मन्थरमीक्षणार्धं
मन्दालसं च मकरालयकन्यकायाः॥

Prāptaṁ padaṁ prathamataḥ khalu yat-prabhāvān
Māṅgalya-bhāji madhu-māthini manmathena;
Mayy āpatet tad iha mantharam īkṣaṇārdhaṁ
Mandālasaṁ ca makarālaya-kanyakāyāḥ.

Meaning in short

May that slow and languid half-glance of the ocean’s daughter, by whose power Manmatha first gained a footing in the auspicious Vishnu, the crusher of Madhu, fall upon me here.

Notes on each word
प्राप्तम्Prāptam

From pra and āp, to reach or gain. It is a past participle: “has been gained”. It goes with the next word: a footing has been gained.

पदम्Padam

Pada is a foot, a step, and so a place to stand, a position. Here it is the footing that Manmatha gained.

प्रथमतःPrathamataḥ

Prathama is first; the ending -taḥ gives “from the first, at first”. It says that this was where it began.

खलुKhalu

A small word for emphasis: “indeed, surely”.

यत्प्रभावात्Yat-prabhāvāt

Yat is “which” and points forward to tad īkṣaṇārdham, “that half-glance”, in the second line: it is the glance whose power is meant. Prabhāva is power, might, influence (from pra and bhū, to come forth). The ending means “by”. Before the next word, which begins with m, the final t becomes n: prabhāvān māṅgalya-.

माङ्गल्यभाजिMāṅgalya-bhāji

Māṅgalya is auspiciousness, good fortune; bhāj means sharing in, having a part of. So he is the auspicious one. The ending means “in”.

मधुमाथिनिMadhu-māthini

Madhu is the name of a demon in the Vishnu tradition; māthin is one who churns or crushes (from math). So this is Vishnu. The next word tells who found a footing in him. The ending means “in”.

मन्मथेनManmathena

Manmatha is a name of Kāma, the god of love and desire. A traditional explanation is that it means “one who churns the mind” (manas, mind, and matha, churning). The ending means “by”: he is the one who gained the footing.

मयिMayi

“In me, on me”. Before āpatet it is written mayy.

आपतेत्Āpatet

From ā (towards) and pat (to fall): to alight, to come down upon. The ending makes it a wish: “may it fall”. The poet’s one request.

तत्Tat

It points to the half-glance of the second line, whose power the first line has just described.

इहIha

“Here, in this place”. He asks that it fall here, upon him as he stands.

मन्थरम्Mantharam

Manthara describes a slow, unhurried movement; in Sanskrit it can also mean lingering or dull. Here it is the quality he asks of the glance: slow, not quick.

ईक्षणार्धम्Īkṣaṇārdham

Īkṣaṇa is a look, a glance, from īkṣ, to see; ardha is half. The words join as īkṣaṇārdham. The same word was used in Verse 3.

मन्दालसम्Mandālasam

Manda is slow, gentle; ālasa is languid, unhurried, as though relaxed and half asleep. The words join as mandālasam. It adds a softer, more drowsy slowness to mantharam.

चCa

Joins the two descriptions of the glance.

मकरालयकन्यकायाःMakarālaya-kanyakāyāḥ

Makara is a sea creature of Indian myth, often shown like a crocodile; ālaya is an abode. So makarālaya is “the home of the makara”: the ocean. Kanyakā is a girl, a daughter. The ending means “of”: she is Lakshmi, born from the ocean.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, your glance once did something wonderful: through its power the god of love first found a footing in Vishnu himself. May that same glance, slow and soft and only half-given, fall on me.” The poet shows what her glance can do, and then asks for it in its smallest form.

From the text

What the verse actually says

The verse is one sentence in two parts. The first part says what the glance has done: by its power Manmatha first gained a footing in Vishnu. The second part is the wish: may that slow, languid half-glance of the ocean’s daughter fall on me here. The word yat (“by whose”) ties the two together: it is the glance itself, not only the Goddess, whose power is meant.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Picture the scene the verse describes. Vishnu sits serene and complete in his own abode, and Lakshmi is beside him. Somewhere in this world is Manmatha, the god of love, with his bow of sugarcane and his arrows of flowers, the picture tradition gives of desire and attraction. Then the poet makes a quiet claim: it was by the power of her glance that even Manmatha first gained a footing in Vishnu.

Notice the turn. Verses 1 to 6 showed us the beauty of her glance; now we see its influence. And then comes the most delicate part of the verse. Having shown how great the power is, the poet asks for the smallest piece of it: mantharam īkṣaṇārdham, a slow half-glance.

Imagine standing before a great queen. She need not speak or make a grand gesture; a small movement of her eyes is enough to say that you have been seen and welcomed. That is the feeling of this verse. The scene is an imagined picture to help us enter the verse, not something the verse itself states in these words. The poet does not demand attention and does not claim a right. He asks that even a fraction of her loving attention fall on him, and in that there is both humility and a great confidence in grace.

Manmatha and Vishnu

Manmatha is the god of love, shown with a bow of sugarcane and arrows of flowers. The verse says that through the power of her glance he first gained a place in Vishnu. A common reading is that her look is what made love take hold in the Lord. The verse does not tell the story, and commentators read it a little differently, but the point is the same: if her glance can move even Vishnu, its power is beyond doubt. Note that the page says “a footing in Vishnu”, not “in his heart”: the verse says padam, a place or footing, and leaves the place unnamed.

Listen

The sound of the verse

Listen for the sound m. Eight words or word-parts begin with ma- or mā-: māṅgalya, madhu, māthini, manmathena, mayy, mantharam, mandālasam, makarālaya. There is also a small play on words: madhu-māthin and manmatha both contain the root math, to churn or crush. One is the crusher of Madhu, and the other, by the traditional explanation, churns the mind.

Three kinds of slowness

The glance is described three ways. Mantharam is slow, unhurried. Mandālasam is slow and languid, relaxed to the point of drowsiness. Īkṣaṇārdham is only half a glance. Nothing in these words is quick, strong or complete. The poet asks for a look that is gentle and small, and trusts that it is enough.

The thread

An echo of Verse 3

The half-glance īkṣaṇārdham is not new. In Verse 3 the poet asked that it rest on him for a moment. Here the same half-glance returns with a reason: now we have been told what it can do. After hearing of its power, the request for just a little of it carries more weight.

Asking from what has been seen

The shape of the argument is simple. The glance gave Manmatha a place in Vishnu, so surely it can do something for me. That is how many prayers work: not by claiming a right, but by pointing to what grace has already done and asking for a share. As a reflection: humility can be strong in this way. It does not demand; it remembers.

The daughter of the ocean

Makarālaya-kanyakā is another name for Lakshmi as the ocean’s daughter, as in Verse 2. In the tradition she rose from the churning of the ocean. The ocean is a picture of depth and hidden treasure, and the verse, which began with the power of her glance, ends by naming where she came from. This is a mythological association, not a historical claim.

Desire and its direction

Manmatha stands for desire and attraction. The verse does not condemn him; it says her glance gave him a footing in Vishnu. As a reflection, and not a statement of the verse: desire is a strong force, and the question is what guides it. The wish to earn can become the wish to create something of value. The wish for recognition can become the wish to do work that matters.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Prabhāva is power, influence. Manmatha is the god of love, “the mind-churner”. Manthara is slow, unhurried.

Say it aloud

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1ReciteSay slowly, mayy āpatet tad iha mantharam īkṣaṇārdham, and let the words settle.
  2. Minute 2PictureLakshmi seated beside Vishnu, and her gaze, slow and kind, turning gently.
  3. Minute 3SilenceSit quietly and ask for nothing yet. Simply stand before her in your mind.
  4. Minute 4Examine desireAsk yourself, “Which of my ambitions deserve my energy? Which need to be refined, or let go?”
  5. Minute 5Offer a prayer. Say silently“O Mother Lakshmi, may your grace guide my wishes. May I work with care, stay humble in success and use whatever I gain for the good of others.”

Reflection for today

  • InfluenceWho has changed my course by a small, kind act, and have I done that for someone?
  • HumilityWhen I ask for something, do I remember what I have already received?
  • DesireWhich of my wishes would I choose again if I looked at them slowly?

One honest answer is enough for today.

The poet first shows the power of her glance, powerful enough to give the god of love a footing in Vishnu. Then he asks only for the smallest, slowest portion of it. The thought to carry is that grace need not be loud to be strong, and that asking quietly is a way of trusting it.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
