The verse

॥ श्लोक १४ ॥

नमोऽस्तु देव्यै भृगुनन्दनायै
नमोऽस्तु विष्णोरुरसि स्थितायै।
नमोऽस्तु लक्ष्म्यै कमलालयायै
नमोऽस्तु दामोदरवल्लभायै॥

॥ श्री महालक्ष्म्यै नमः ॥

Namo ’stu devyai bhṛgu-nandanāyai
Namo ’stu viṣṇor urasi sthitāyai;
Namo ’stu lakṣmyai kamalālayāyai
Namo ’stu dāmodara-vallabhāyai.

Meaning in short

Let there be a bow to the Goddess, the daughter of Bhṛgu. Let there be a bow to her who rests on the chest of Vishnu. Let there be a bow to Lakṣmī, whose home is the lotus. Let there be a bow to her who is the beloved of Dāmodara.

Four bows that say where she is from, where she rests, where she lives and to whom she belongs.

Key highlights

  • Bhṛgu Birth: the daughter of the sage Bhṛgu
  • Urasi sthitā Place: she rests on Vishnu’s chest
  • Kamalālayā Home: the lotus is her dwelling
  • Dāmodara Beloved: of the rope-bellied Vishnu

BhṛguBirth

Bhṛgu is an ancient sage, and nandanā is a daughter. The tradition says Lakshmi was first born as his daughter and later rose again from the ocean of milk. A reflection: she is honoured both for a human family and for a cosmic birth.

Pause and reflect: Whom do I thank for what I have become?

Urasi sthitāPlace

Urasi sthitā means resting on the chest. Pictures of Vishnu show a mark and a jewel on his chest where she is said to dwell. A reflection: in this picture wealth rests near the heart, not at a distance from it.

Pause and reflect: Is my work kept close to what I value?

KamalālayāHome

Kamalālayā means whose home is the lotus. In Verse 13 the lotus was her seat; here it is her home. A seat is where you sit, a home is where you belong.

Pause and reflect: Where in my life do I feel at home?

DāmodaraBeloved

Dāmodara means “with a rope round the belly”, a name from the story of child Krishna bound by his foster mother. The poet picks Vishnu’s gentlest form. His name changes from verse to verse while she stays the same.

Pause and reflect: To whom or what do I owe my best effort?

Notes on each word
नमः अस्तुNamo ’stu

As in the verses before, namaḥ (a bow) and astu (let it be) join into namo ’stu. It opens every line, so the verse is four bows said in one breath.

देव्यैDevyai

Devī is a goddess, the feminine of deva, a god. The ending -yai means “to”: a feminine word ending in a long ī takes -yai in this form, so devī becomes devyai. Here “the Goddess” is simply her title, before the other descriptions are added.

भृगुनन्दनायैBhṛgu-nandanāyai

Bhṛgu is the name of an ancient sage. Nandana is a child, literally “one who delights”, and the feminine nandanā is a daughter. So she is the daughter of Bhṛgu. The Purāṇic tradition says that Lakshmi was first born as the daughter of Bhṛgu, and later rose again from the ocean of milk.

विष्णोरुरसि स्थितायैViṣṇor urasi sthitāyai

Viṣṇoḥ is “of Vishnu”; urasi is “on the chest”, from uras, chest; sthitā is “standing, staying, resting”. The visarga of viṣṇoḥ becomes r before the vowel of urasi, so the words read viṣṇor urasi. She is the one who rests on Vishnu’s chest, close to his heart.

लक्ष्म्यैLakṣmyai

Lakṣmī is the Goddess’s own name, from a root meaning “to see, to mark”: good fortune that is visible. As a feminine word ending in a long ī, it takes -yai for “to”: lakṣmyai. After three lines of descriptions, the poet finally bows to her by name.

कमलालयायैKamala-ālayāyai

Kamala is a lotus; ālaya is a home, a dwelling. So she is “the one whose home is the lotus”. The a of kamala and the ā of ālaya join into ā: kamalālaya. The same idea was in Verse 5, where she is called lotus-dwelling.

दामोदरवल्लभायैDāmodara-vallabhāyai

Dāmodara is a name of Vishnu in his Krishna form: dāman is a rope and udara is the belly, “the one with a rope round his belly”, from the story of the child Krishna tied by his foster mother. Vallabhā is a beloved. So she is the beloved of Dāmodara.

Sanskrit structure

How the verse is built. Grammar only, with no interpretation.

Anvaya The sentence in plain order

Each line is namo ’stu followed by one description in the “to” form (the dative). The word tasyai, “to her”, is understood. In the second line the description is three words: “of Vishnu, on the chest, to her who stays”. The lines already stand in prose order.

Vibhakti Why the endings are -yai and -āyai

Namaḥ, “a bow”, takes the “to” form, the dative (Pāṇini 2.3.16). A feminine word in long -ī, such as devī or lakṣmī, takes -yai: devyai, lakṣmyai. A feminine word in -ā takes -āyai: nandanāyai, sthitāyai, ālayāyai, vallabhāyai.

The second line adds two other cases. Viṣṇoḥ is the “of” form (genitive) of Viṣṇu, and urasi is the “on” form (locative) of uras, chest: “on the chest of Vishnu”. The visarga of viṣṇoḥ becomes r before the vowel of urasi.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, I bow to you, the Goddess, daughter of Bhṛgu. I bow to you who rest on Vishnu’s chest. I bow to you, Lakṣmī, who live in the lotus. I bow to you, beloved of Dāmodara.” Four bows, one in each line.

From the text

What the verse actually says

As in the two verses before it, each line is namo ’stu followed by one description of the Goddess in the “to” form. The four are: the Goddess, daughter of Bhṛgu; she who rests on the chest of Vishnu; Lakṣmī, whose home is the lotus; and the beloved of Dāmodara. This is the verse in which the poet says her name, Lakṣmī, aloud.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

The verse reads like a family record. Her birth: a sage’s daughter. Her place: against her husband’s heart. Her home: a flower. Her belonging: she is Dāmodara’s own. Together they say where she comes from, where she rests, where she lives and to whom she belongs.

Bhṛgu-nandanā, daughter of the sage

Bhṛgu is one of the great sages in the Purāṇas. The tradition, for example the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, tells that Lakshmi was born to Bhṛgu and his wife Khyāti, and was then born again from the churning of the ocean of milk, as Verse 12 hinted. The two stories are told in different places. As a reflection: she is honoured both for a human family and for a cosmic birth.

Urasi sthitā, resting on the chest

In pictures of Vishnu the chest carries a mark called Śrīvatsa and a jewel, the Kaustubha, and the tradition says Lakshmi dwells there. Verse 5 described her glance as a garland over the Kaustubha on that very chest. As a reflection: wealth, in this picture, rests near the heart and not at a distance from it.

Kamalālayā, the lotus as home

The lotus appears again as her place. In Verse 13 it was her golden seat; here it is her home. The same flower is used twice with a different shade: a seat is where you sit, a home is where you belong.

Dāmodara-vallabhā, beloved of Dāmodara

Dāmodara recalls the story in which the child Krishna was tied round the belly by his foster mother Yaśodā, whose ropes kept falling short until he allowed himself to be bound. The poet picks this name for Vishnu in his gentlest form. As a reflection: the husband’s name changes from verse to verse (Puruṣottama, Nārāyaṇa, Śārṅgāyudha, now Dāmodara), while she stays the same.

The thread

How this verse follows the last

Verse 13 told us her seat, her rule and her mercy. This verse tells us her birth, her closeness to Vishnu, her home and her belonging. The poet keeps bowing, and the picture grows more personal each time.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Uras is the chest. Ālaya is a home, a dwelling. Sthitā is one who stands or rests.

Say it aloud

How to pronounce the key words

Namo ’stu: na-mo-stu. Devyai: dev-yai. Bhṛgu-nandanāyai: bhri-gu-nan-da-naa-yai. Viṣṇor urasi sthitāyai: vish-nor u-ra-si sthi-taa-yai. Lakṣmyai: laksh-myai. Kamalālayāyai: ka-ma-laa-la-yaa-yai. Dāmodara-vallabhāyai: daa-mo-da-ra-val-la-bhaa-yai.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1Her birthSay silently, devyai bhṛgu-nandanāyai namaḥ. Think of where you come from, your family and your teachers, and give thanks.
  2. Minute 2Her placeSay viṣṇor urasi sthitāyai namaḥ. Place a hand on your own chest and ask that what you earn stays close to what you value.
  3. Minute 3Her homeSay lakṣmyai kamalālayāyai namaḥ. Picture a lotus and ask where in your life you feel at home.
  4. Minute 4Her belongingSay dāmodara-vallabhāyai namaḥ. Think of one person or purpose you belong to, and give thanks for it.
  5. Minute 5A bowBow your head and say thank you for what you have.

Four bows give the Goddess a birth, a place, a home and a beloved, and in the third line the poet at last bows to her by name. The thought to carry is that fortune, in this picture, is not a stranger: it has a family, a place near the heart and a home.
