The verse

गीर्देवतेति गरुडध्वजसुन्दरीति
शाकम्भरीति शशिशेखरवल्लभेति।
सृष्टिस्थितिप्रलयकेलिषु संस्थितायै
तस्यै नमस्त्रिभुवनैकगुरोस्तरुण्यै॥

Gīrdevateti garuḍadhvaja-sundarīti
Śākambharīti śaśiśekhara-vallabheti;
Sṛṣṭi-sthiti-pralaya-keliṣu saṁsthitāyai
Tasyai namas tribhuvanaika-guros taruṇyai.

Meaning in short

Salutations to her who is called the goddess of speech, the beautiful beloved of him whose banner bears Garuḍa, Śākambharī, and the beloved of the moon-crested one; who abides in the play of creation, preservation and dissolution; to that youthful wife of the one teacher of the three worlds.

Four names, one Goddess, present in the play of creation, preservation and dissolution. The hymn turns from asking to bowing.

Key highlights

  • Gīrdevatā Speech: the goddess of speech, taken as Sarasvatī
  • Sundarī Lakshmi: the beloved of Vishnu, Garuḍa’s banner
  • Śākambharī Nourisher: she who feeds the world
  • Vallabhā Pārvatī: beloved of the moon-crested Shiva

GīrdevatāSpeech

Gīrdevatā is the goddess of speech, usually taken as Sarasvatī. Each name ends with iti, “thus”, like a quotation mark: she who is called this. Traditional readers take these as names of the one Goddess.

Pause and reflect: How do I use the gift of speech today?

SundarīLakshmi

Garuḍadhvaja-sundarī is Vishnu’s beloved, Lakshmi. This site follows the traditional reading that the hymn is addressed to Lakshmi throughout, who also bears these other names.

Pause and reflect: What do I value most in the name Lakshmi: beauty, grace or care?

ŚākambharīNourisher

Śākambharī is the form remembered in the Devī Māhātmya for sustaining the world with greens in a time of drought. After asking for puṣṭi in Verse 9, the poet names the Goddess who feeds the world.

Pause and reflect: Who feeds me, and whom do I feed?

VallabhāPārvatī

Śaśiśekhara-vallabhā is the beloved of Shiva, the one who wears the moon, usually taken as Pārvatī. The Goddess is bowed to as the ever-young wife of the one teacher of the three worlds.

Notes on each word
गीर्देवता इतिGīr-devatā iti

Gīr is speech, voice, the spoken word; devatā is a deity. Tradition understands her as Sarasvatī. Iti means “thus”; it closes a name, as quotation marks do in English. Because ā and i meet, the two words are written together as gīrdevateti.

गरुडध्वजसुन्दरी इतिGaruḍadhvaja-sundarī iti

Garuḍa is the eagle, the mount of Vishnu; dhvaja is a banner, so garuḍadhvaja is the one whose banner bears Garuḍa: Vishnu. Sundarī is a beautiful woman. So she is Vishnu’s beautiful beloved: Lakshmi herself. The words join as sundarīti.

शाकम्भरी इतिŚākambharī iti

Śāka is vegetable, leafy food; bharī is a bearer or supporter (from bhṛ, to carry, to sustain). So śākambharī is the one who sustains life with greens and plants. It is also the name of a form of the Goddess who is remembered for feeding the world (see below). The words join as śākambharīti.

शशिशेखरवल्लभा इतिŚaśiśekhara-vallabhā iti

Śaśin is the moon (“the one with the hare mark”); śekhara is a crest or crown; śaśiśekhara is the one who wears the moon as a crest: Shiva. Vallabhā is a beloved, a wife. The words join as vallabheti, where ā and i become e.

सृष्टिस्थितिप्रलयकेलिषुSṛṣṭi-sthiti-pralaya-keliṣu

Sṛṣṭi is creation, letting go forth; sthiti is standing, staying, preservation; pralaya is dissolution, the melting away of the world at the end of a cycle. Keli is play, sport. The ending means “in”. The three great acts are called play: done freely and without strain.

संस्थितायैSaṁsthitāyai

From sam (together, fully) and sthā (to stand): firmly established, present. The ending means “to”: this is the one being saluted.

तस्यैTasyai

“To that one”. It gathers all the names and descriptions above into one person.

नमःNamaḥ

Before the next word it is written namas. The same word that ends many Sanskrit prayers: “bowing”.

त्रिभुवनैकगुरोःTribhuvana-eka-guroḥ

Tri is three; bhuvana is a world; eka is one, sole; guru is a teacher, one who is weighty with knowledge. The three worlds are heaven, earth and the lower realm. Traditionally the one teacher or lord of the three worlds is Nārāyaṇa. A and e join as ai, giving -bhuvanaika-. The ending means “of”, and the final ḥ becomes s before t: guros taruṇyai.

तरुण्यैTaruṇyai

Taruṇī is a young woman, in the full bloom of youth. The ending means “to”. She is the youthful consort of the guru of the three worlds.

Deeper spiritual insights

Interpretation and reflection: ideas that help us read the verse, not part of the text itself.

In simple words

“O Mother, you are known as Sarasvatī, as Vishnu’s beloved, as Śākambharī and as the beloved of Shiva. You are present whenever the world is made, kept and dissolved. I bow to you, the ever-young wife of the one teacher of the three worlds.” After asking for puṣṭi in Verse 9, the poet stops to bow.

From the text

What the verse actually says

The verse is one long salutation. It names her four times, each name closed with iti: the goddess of speech, the beloved of Garuḍa’s banner, Śākambharī, the beloved of the moon-crested one. It says where she is found: in the play of creation, preservation and dissolution. And it bows to her as the young wife of the teacher of the three worlds.

Picture it

The poetry hidden in the verse

Imagine a devotee standing before a great temple. At first he sees different forms. There is a Goddess with a vīṇā, the lute, who stands for speech, music and learning. There is a Goddess beside Vishnu, who stands for beauty, prosperity and keeping. There is a Mother surrounded by fruit, vegetables and green leaves, who feeds the world. And there is a Goddess beside Shiva. The names differ, the appearances differ, and so do their relationships.

Then the poet invites us to look beyond the forms. By the traditional reading, the power that nourishes, creates, sustains and transforms the universe is one, and the verse bows to her under all these names.

See how the verse moves: it begins with the personal, the beloved of Vishnu, and widens to the universal, the one present in making, keeping and dissolving the world. The temple scene is a picture to help us read the verse; the verse itself gives only the names and the bow.

What iti is doing

Each name ends with iti, meaning “thus”. It works like an opening and closing quotation mark: she who is called gīrdevatā, she who is called śākambharī. The poet is repeating the names by which she is known, not defining her by any one.

The names

Gīrdevatā is the goddess of speech, usually taken as Sarasvatī. Garuḍadhvaja-sundarī is Vishnu’s beloved, Lakshmi. Śākambharī is a form of the Mother remembered for feeding the world. Śaśiśekhara-vallabhā is the beloved of Shiva, usually taken as Pārvatī. Traditional readers understand these as names of the one Goddess; others take the verse as a salutation to several goddesses together. This site follows the first, because the hymn is addressed to Lakshmi throughout.

Śākambharī

In the Devī Māhātmya, a major text of the Goddess tradition, she promises that in a time of drought she will sustain the world with life-giving vegetables from her own body, and she is then known as Śākambharī. The name śāka-bhṛ means exactly that: the bearer of greens. It is a good companion to the previous verse: after asking for puṣṭi, nourishment, the poet names the Goddess who feeds the world.

Creation, preservation, dissolution as play

Keli means play. Śaṅkara does not call the making, keeping and ending of the world her labour; he calls it her play. As a reflection, this does not make dissolution gentle or trivial; it says that nothing in the cycle lies outside her. Compare līlā in the glossary, the same idea of graceful, unforced action.

Who is the guru of the three worlds?

The verse says only tribhuvanaika-guru, the one teacher of the three worlds. Commentators usually understand this as Vishnu, Lakshmi’s husband, which suits the second name in the list. The poet ends by returning to the personal: after all the cosmic names, she is a young wife.

Youth

Taruṇī is a young woman. In Sanskrit poetry it often stands for freshness and bloom. The tradition does not say the Goddess has an age; it says she is ever fresh. A reflection: creation and ending may go on through ages, but the poet thinks of her as new.

The turn to salutation

From this verse the hymn moves from asking to bowing: namas is the heart of this verse, and a run of salutations begins here and carries on in the verses that follow. The poet has asked; now he honours.

Words to keep

Three Sanskrit words to keep

Śākambharī is the one who sustains with greens. Pralaya is dissolution, the end of a cycle. Sthiti is staying, keeping, preservation.

Say it aloud

How to pronounce the key words

Gīrdevateti: geer-de-va-te-ti. Garuḍadhvaja-sundarīti: ga-ru-da-dhva-ja-sun-da-ree-ti. Śākambharīti: shaa-kam-ba-ree-ti. Śaśiśekhara-vallabheti: sha-shi-she-kha-ra-val-la-bhe-ti. Sṛṣṭi-sthiti-pralaya-keliṣu: sri-shti-sthi-ti-pra-la-ya-ke-li-shu. Saṁsthitāyai: sam-sthi-taa-yai. Tasyai namas: tas-yai na-mas. Tribhuvanaika-guros: tri-bhu-va-nai-ka-gu-ros. Taruṇyai: ta-run-yai.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1SettleSit quietly and recite the first line slowly, gīrdevateti garuḍadhvaja-sundarīti, once or twice.
  2. Minute 2SpeechThink of the Goddess as the power of speech. Resolve to speak truthfully and kindly today.
  3. Minute 3NourishmentPicture a green field and a full plate. Give thanks, silently, for the food in front of you and for the people who grew it.
  4. Minute 4EndingsBring to mind one thing that is coming to an end in your life, a habit, a worry or a season. Let it go with respect.
  5. Minute 5Offer a prayer. Say silently“O Mother, you are the word, the harvest and the turning of all things. May I speak well, be grateful for what feeds me, and meet change with trust.” If you wish, bow your head at the end.

Reflection for today

  • SpeechHave my words been truthful and kind today?
  • FoodDo I remember what sustains me and who made it?
  • ChangeWhat ending am I resisting that may be making room for something new?

One honest answer is enough for today.

The poet has looked into her eyes for nine verses. Now he steps back and bows to everything she is: speech, beauty, food, the beloved of two great gods and the play of the whole world. The thought to carry is that prosperity is not separate from knowledge, nourishment and change; the Mother who gives wealth is also the one who feeds, teaches and renews.

This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
