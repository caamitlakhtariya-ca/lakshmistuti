The tradition behind the hymn

The story of the Kanakadhāra Stotram

A young renunciate, a poor woman and one gooseberry. This page tells the traditional story in full, explains the name of the hymn, and draws out what the story teaches.

Who was Ādi Śaṅkarācārya?

Ādi Śaṅkarācārya is the great teacher of Advaita Vedānta, the philosophy of non-duality. He is traditionally placed in Kerala in the 8th century, and the tradition says he took to the life of a renunciate while still very young. The Kanakadhāra Stotram is traditionally attributed to him. Scholars debate the authorship of many works that carry his name, so this page calls the account below what it is: the traditional story.

The story

A young seeker at the door

As a student and renunciate, the young Śaṅkara went from house to house asking for bhikṣā, a small offering of food. In most tellings he is about eight years old. The custom is not really about need. Accepting whatever is freely offered teaches humility to the receiver and gives the giver a chance to give.

One small gooseberry

One day he stopped at the home of a poor Brahmin family. The lady of the house searched every corner of her kitchen and found nothing to offer, except one dried āmalakī, an Indian gooseberry, the last edible thing in the house. She had to choose between keeping it for her hungry family and giving it to a guest. She gave it, wholeheartedly.

A hymn is born

Śaṅkara saw at once how poor she was, and how generous. Moved, he composed a hymn in praise of Goddess Lakshmi, praying that her compassionate glance fall on this household. The hymn is the Kanakadhāra Stotram.

Karma, and an appeal to compassion

In one version, the Goddess answers that the family has not yet earned wealth through past actions. Śaṅkara then appeals not to justice but to compassion, and the story can be read as carrying a quiet argument: a woman who gives everything she has, has she not just earned merit this very moment?

The stream of gold

The Goddess is pleased, and golden gooseberries fall on the house. Kanaka means gold and dhārā means a stream, so Kanakadhārā is “the stream of gold”.

Tellings differ on the details, such as the exact place, the family and what the family did afterwards. Whether it happened exactly so is for each reader to weigh. What is clear is why the story has lasted.

What the story teaches

  • She gave before she received. The miracle begins with her generosity, not with the Goddess.
  • Wealth is more than money. The hymn asks for the grace that lets a life flourish: health, good company, peace of mind, and enough.
  • Humility on both sides. A seeker who accepts, a householder who gives, and neither is small.

Where to go in the hymn

The hymn is easiest to read with the story in mind. Begin with Verse 1. Verse 11 is a good one for the question of how prosperity is built, and Verse 16 for seeking the Mother’s grace while doing so. The closing verses, 21 and 22, are a plea for her favour and a promise to those who recite the hymn.
