Lakshmi Aarti – meaning and how to perform it

The Lakshmi Aarti, “Om Jai Lakshmi Mata”, is the song of light sung to Goddess Lakshmi at the end of worship, above all on the evening of Diwali. This page explains how the aarti is performed at home and what its stanzas say.

By Amit N. Lakhtariya

A note on the words: the aarti is a sung folk hymn, and printed versions differ slightly from region to region and family to family. This page explains it and does not print the words, so please use the version your family or temple sings.

What is an aarti?

An aarti is a short act of worship in which a lit lamp is waved before the deity while a hymn is sung. The word is usually traced to the Sanskrit ārātrika, connected with the night and with the light that drives away darkness. At the end the flame is shared: people pass their hands over it and bring them to the eyes and forehead, taking the blessing of the light.

The Lakshmi Aarti is sung on Fridays in many homes, and above all during the Lakshmi Puja of Diwali. The date and the auspicious time (muhūrta) of the evening puja change every year, so check a local panchang or your temple for the exact time.

How to perform it at home

There is no single rule, and families differ. This is a common simple order.

  1. Prepare the place. Clean the space and set an image or idol of the Goddess on a clean cloth. Many people draw a small rangoli at the entrance.
  2. Prepare the thali. A plate with a ghee lamp (dīpa), incense, flowers, kumkum, and a small piece of camphor or a second lamp for the aarti. Keep the sweets or fruit that will be offered close by.
  3. Complete the puja first. The aarti closes the worship. Offer the flowers, kumkum and sweets before you begin.
  4. Light the lamp. Hold the thali in your right hand, with the left hand free to ring a small bell if you have one.
  5. Wave the light. While the hymn is sung, move the thali slowly in a clockwise circle in front of the image. Some families make seven turns, some three, some wave it to the feet, the middle and the face in turn. Follow your own tradition.
  6. Take the aarti. When the hymn ends, pass your hands over the flame and touch them to your eyes and forehead. Then offer the prasad.
  7. Stay for a moment. A minute of quiet gratitude closes the aarti well.

Safety: keep long sleeves, dupattas and curtains away from the flame, and keep children at a distance from the lit thali.

What the aarti says, stanza by stanza

The aarti has a refrain and eight short stanzas. Below, each is explained in simple words. These are my own explanations of the hymn’s ideas, and the practical lessons are reflections inspired by it, not literal lines in the text.

The refrain: gratitude

Every stanza returns to the same cry, Om Jai Lakshmi Mata, “Victory to Mother Lakshmi”. Repeating it is a way of saying thank you: for food on the table, for work, for family, for health and for the chance to be useful. Lakshmi is felt differently by different people: as honest profit by a trader, as a good harvest by a farmer, as understanding by a student, as a settled and happy household by a parent. Having wealth and understanding what wealth is for are two different things.

First stanza: served by Vishnu and the creator

The aarti opens by placing Lakshmi beside Hari Vishnu, the preserver of the world, and the creator, who serve her night and day. Tradition pairs Lakshmi, who stands for prosperity, with Vishnu, who stands for order and right conduct. The quiet lesson is that wealth and dharma should walk together. Earning money is an achievement; keeping it responsibly and using it for something worthwhile is the larger task.

Second stanza: one mother, many names

Here she is addressed as Umā, Ramā and Brahmāṇī, names usually linked with Pārvatī, Lakshmi and the power of Brahmā (often joined with Sarasvatī), and as the mother of the world. The sun and moon meditate on her and the sage Nārada sings of her. Readers take the stanza to say that the great goddesses are not rivals but many faces of one divine feminine.

Third stanza: the stainless Durgā

She is called Durgā in form, spotless, and the giver of comfort and wealth; whoever meditates on her receives success and prosperity. It is a reminder that Lakshmi is not only gentle. Prosperity also needs strength: the courage to face a bad year, the patience to wait through uncertainty and the character to stay honest under pressure. A person who keeps their calm and their integrity in a crisis holds a wealth that does not appear on a balance sheet.

Fourth stanza: from the unseen to the seen

The stanza names her as the one who dwells in the lower worlds, the giver of good, the one who lights up the working of karma, and the rescuer from the ocean of worldly life. One reading is that fortune and the results of our actions are not separate: effort and conduct matter, and grace works alongside them, not instead of them.

Fifth stanza: the true wealth of a home

Perhaps the most practical stanza. It says that where she lives, all good qualities come, everything becomes possible and the mind is no longer anxious. The emphasis is on virtues (sadguṇa), not on gold. A home with plenty of money but no trust is not a happy one, while a home where elders are respected, children learn good values, guests are welcomed and the family stands together has a deep kind of wealth. This does not mean good values remove every money problem; they give lasting prosperity a firmer base.

Sixth stanza: nothing happens without her

Without her, the stanza says, sacrifices would not be performed, no one would find clothing, and the plenty of food and drink would not exist. Behind a single meal stand farmers, nature, transporters and traders; behind a business stand employees, customers and suppliers. The stanza invites a question: if I have received prosperity, am I also adding something worthwhile to other people’s lives? Paying people fairly, dealing honestly and helping those in need are ways to keep worshipping after the aarti ends.

Seventh stanza: born of the milk ocean

She is praised as the home of good qualities, beautiful, and born of the ocean of milk. Without her, the stanza says, the fourteen jewels of that churning could not be had. It points to the old story of the churning of the ocean, in which Lakshmi rose from the waters. The same story lies behind her name Sāgarasambhavā in Verse 2 of the Kanakadhāra Stotram.

Final stanza: the fruit of singing

The closing stanza says that whoever sings this aarti to Mahālakṣmī finds joy filling the heart and sin falling away. Singing alone does not cancel the results of what we do, and devotion is not an exemption from responsibility. What it can do is help us see our own faults, such as greed, pride, resentment, fear and ingratitude, and find the strength to turn back to the right path. The deeper fruit of prayer is not always that life changes as we wish; it can be that we gain the wisdom to live rightly whatever comes.

A closing prayer

After the aarti, many people add a few words of their own. Here is one, in plain English.

O Mother Mahalakshmi, give us enough wealth to live with dignity and to meet our duties, and the wisdom never to let the pursuit of wealth make us forget our dharma. Give us courage in hard times and humility when success comes. Bless our homes with food, peace and understanding, and may we never waste the food you give. When we succeed, let us stay grateful; when we fail, let us keep our courage; when we receive, let us learn to share. Make us worthy keepers of the prosperity we ask for.

One thought to take away

We often pray for Lakshmi to enter our homes. We might also ask whether we are building a home worthy of the qualities she stands for: gratitude in place of complaint, integrity in place of greed, love in place of ego, humility in place of pride, and a heart that remembers those who have less. Money can build a house, but values make it a home. Money can buy comfort but cannot guarantee contentment. May we receive prosperity with gratitude, keep it with wisdom, use it rightly and share it with compassion.
