Mahālakṣmī Aṣṭakam – Verse 1

Namas te ’stu Mahāmāye – the first bow

By Amit N. Lakhtariya

Devanāgarī

नमस्तेऽस्तु महामाये श्रीपीठे सुरपूजिते।
शङ्खचक्रगदाहस्ते महालक्ष्मि नमोऽस्तु ते॥

Namas te 'stu mahāmāye śrīpīṭhe sura-pūjite,
Śaṅkha-cakra-gadā-haste mahālakṣmi namo 'stu te.

Word by wordMeaningDeeper insightsMeditationReflection

Word-by-word meaning

नमः ते अस्तुNamaḥ te astu

Salutation to you; may there be a bow to you

Namaḥ is a bow or salutation; te is “to you”; astu is “let it be”.

महामायेMahāmāye

O Great Māyā, great divine power

Māyā is the creative power by which the world appears. Here it is a title of honour, not a charge of deception.

श्रीपीठेŚrīpīṭhe

O you who are seated at Śrīpīṭha, the seat of Śrī

Pīṭha is a seat or sacred centre. Tradition links this seat with Kolhapur; the phrase can also be read simply as “seat of prosperity”.

सुरपूजितेSura-pūjite

Worshipped by the gods

Sura is a god; pūjita is worshipped.

शङ्खचक्रगदाहस्तेŚaṅkha-cakra-gadā-haste

Holding conch, discus and mace in her hands

Śaṅkha is the conch, cakra the discus, gadā the mace, hasta the hand.

महालक्ष्मिMahālakṣmi

O Mahalakshmi

The vocative of Mahālakṣmī, “the Great Lakshmi”.

नमः अस्तु तेNamo ’stu te

My salutations to you

The refrain that closes every verse of the hymn.

Full meaning

Salutations to you, O Mahāmāyā, great divine power, seated at Śrīpīṭha and worshipped by the gods; you who hold the conch, discus and mace in your hands. O Mahalakshmi, I bow to you.

Deeper spiritual insights

In simple words

The hymn opens with a plain bow. Before asking for anything, the poet greets the Goddess with three titles: the great creative power, the one seated at the sacred seat of Śrī, and the one whom even the gods worship. Reverence comes first; everything else follows from it.

Who is speaking

Tradition ascribes this hymn to Indra, king of the gods, and places it in the Padma Purāṇa. Accounts of the occasion differ, so it is safer to hold only the broad idea: even a king of the gods approaches Lakshmi with a bow.

Conch, discus and mace

These are the familiar emblems of Lord Vishnu. Here the Goddess carries them herself. The picture is of a Lakshmi who is not only gentle and generous but also powerful. That is a useful balance to keep in mind through the rest of the hymn.

Mahāmāyā is not “illusion” only

In popular speech māyā often means illusion. In devotional Sanskrit it also means the creative power through which the world is manifest. Read that way, mahāmāyā addresses Lakshmi as the great power behind creation.

Practical meditation

  1. Minute 1: sit quietly and let the breath settle.
  2. Minute 2: recite the first line slowly, feeling the bow in namaḥ.
  3. Minute 3: picture the Goddess holding the conch, discus and mace, calm and capable.
  4. Minute 4: offer one silent thanks for something you already have.

Reflection for today

Before I ask for anything today, can I begin by acknowledging what I have already received? What would change in my day if I started with a bow instead of a request?

The first verse is a salutation and nothing more, and that is its strength. A hymn that begins with reverence trains the mind to receive before it asks. This meditation is a devotional practice for attention and reflection; it does not promise a supernatural result or guarantee material wealth.
